Glossary

  • Aleatoric probability: Probability interpreted as the relative frequency at which different values would be observed across all the possible replications of the same experiment or observation protocol. It relates to measurement error and to stochasticity and variability in ecological systems. This is the interpretation of probability used in this course.

  • Asymptotically normal: An estimator is asymptotically normal when its sampling distribution approaches a Normal distribution as the sample size grows. For the mean of i.i.d. observations from a distribution with finite variance, this follows from the central limit theorem.

  • Bandwidth: The standard deviation of the kernel used in a kernel density estimate. It plays the same role as the bin width of a histogram: a small bandwidth gives a wiggly estimate and a large bandwidth a very smooth one.

  • Bias: The difference between the mean of the sampling distribution of an estimator and the parameter that it estimates, \[ \operatorname{Bias}(\hat\theta) = \mathbb{E}[\hat\theta] - \theta. \] An estimator is unbiased when its bias is zero. Bias is a property of the whole sampling distribution: individual estimates of an unbiased estimator still fall above or below the parameter.

  • Central limit theorem: The mean of \(n\) i.i.d. observations from a distribution with finite variance has a sampling distribution that approaches a Normal distribution as \(n\) grows, regardless of the distribution of the individual observations.

  • Central moment: An expectation used to describe features of a distribution. The \(k\)th central moment is \(\mathbb{E}[(X-\mathbb{E}[X])^k]\). Variance is the central moment for k = 2.

  • Confidence interval: A range of values for a parameter, calculated from a sample with a procedure that has a known behaviour across repeated hypothetical experiments. Once an interval has been calculated, it either contains the parameter or it does not, so the confidence level is a property of the procedure and not a probability that the parameter lies in that particular interval.

  • Confidence level: The fraction of repeated hypothetical experiments for which a confidence interval calculated with a given procedure would contain the parameter (e.g., 95%). The actual fraction, the coverage, may differ from the intended level when the procedure relies on approximations.

  • Consistency: An estimator \(\hat\theta_n\) calculated from a sample of size \(n\) is consistent if it converges in probability to the parameter \(\theta\), that is, for any positive margin \(\varepsilon\), \[ \lim_{n \to \infty} P\left(\left|\hat\theta_n - \theta\right| \ge \varepsilon\right)=0. \] It describes the probability of large errors, not the ordering of the estimates of two particular samples.

  • Continuous random variable: A random variable that can take any value within an interval of the real numbers (e.g., all positive numbers, or all numbers between 0 and 1). Its values cannot be listed one by one, the probability of any single value is zero, and its distribution assigns probabilities to intervals through a probability density function.

  • Correlation: A parameter of a joint distribution, such as the bivariate Normal distribution, that controls how strongly two random variables change together. For a bivariate Normal distribution, a correlation of zero means that the two variables are independent.

  • Coverage: The fraction of repeated hypothetical experiments for which the confidence interval calculated with a given procedure contains the parameter. It can be checked by simulation when the parameter that generated the samples is known.

  • Cumulative probability function: The function \(F_X(x)=P(X \le x)\), the probability that a random variable takes a value at or below \(x\). It is calculated by adding probability masses (discrete) or by integrating the density (continuous), it is always between zero and one, and it never decreases as \(x\) increases. In R, it is implemented by the p* functions.

  • Data-generating process: The mechanism that produces observations in an experiment, survey or any other relevant research protocol. It includes ecological or physical processes, systematic relationships among variables, variation among units, measurement and sampling procedures, and observational error. A statistical model is a simplified representation of this process.

  • Density-scaled histogram: A histogram in which the height of each bar is the relative frequency of the bin divided by the width of the bin. The area of each bar equals the relative frequency and the areas of all bars add up to one, so the histogram can be compared with a probability density function.

  • Discrete random variable: A random variable that takes values from a finite or countably infinite set, such as the counts \(0, 1, 2, \ldots\). A set is finite when it has a fixed maximum number of values (e.g., 0 to 4 surviving seeds out of four) and countably infinite when there is no pre-specified maximum but the values can still be listed (e.g., any non-negative integer).

  • Dispersion ratio: The ratio of the sample variance to the sample mean. For independent counts from a Poisson distribution it tends to be close to one, so a substantially larger ratio suggests overdispersion. It is an exploratory diagnostic and does not identify the cause of the extra variation.

  • Empirical cumulative distribution: The cumulative function of the empirical distribution: the fraction of observations at or below each value. It requires neither bins nor a bandwidth and, for continuous data, it increases by \(1/n\) at each observed value. In R, it is calculated with ecdf().

  • Empirical distribution: The distribution represented directly by observed data, by assuming each possible observation has a probability mass equal to its relative frequency in the sample. It describes the observed sample in a way that we can compute quantiles and moments, it is not a description of the data generating process.

  • Epistemic probability: Probability interpreted as how certain we are about each possible value when predicting an outcome, which depends on the knowledge or information that we have about the system.

  • Estimate: The value that an estimator returns for the sample that we actually observed. It is marked with a hat, e.g., \(\hat\lambda = 4\).

  • Estimator: A rule (i.e., a function) that we apply to a sample to calculate a value for a parameter. Since we treat the observations as random variables, an estimator is also a random variable: a different sample would give a different estimate.

  • Expectation: An operator that maps a function of a random variable to its probability-weighted average under a specified distribution. For a function \(f(X)\),

\[ \mathbb{E}[f(X)] = \begin{cases} \sum_x f(x)\,P(X=x), & \text{for a discrete random variable},\\ \int f(x)f_X(x)\,dx, & \text{for a continuous random variable}, \end{cases} \]

  • Four faces of a distribution: The four functions that every distribution has: the probability mass (or density), the cumulative probability, the quantile function and the random sampler. In R they are implemented by functions with the prefixes d, p, q and r, followed by a short name for the distribution (e.g., dbinom, pbinom, qbinom and rbinom).

  • Glivenko–Cantelli theorem: The empirical cumulative distribution of an i.i.d. sample converges uniformly (that is, for all values at once) to the cumulative distribution of the model as the sample size grows. It extends the law of large numbers, which applies to the relative frequency of one event at a time.

  • Histogram: A summary of a sample of continuous measurements. The range of the measurements is divided into intervals (called bins) and the observations in each bin are counted. Dividing each count by the sample size gives the relative frequency of the bin. The width of the bins is a choice that changes how the same sample looks.

  • Independence: Two random variables are independent if knowledge about one of them does not change the probabilities for the other. Equivalently, their joint distribution is the product of their marginal distributions, \(P(X_1=x_1, X_2=x_2)=P(X_1=x_1)P(X_2=x_2)\) for discrete variables and \(f_{X,Y}(x,y)=f_X(x)f_Y(y)\) for continuous variables. It is a modelling assumption: it is not guaranteed merely because observations were collected at different locations or times.

  • Independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.): A set of random variables are i.i.d. when each of them follows the same distribution and the value of one does not change the probabilities for the others.

  • Joint distribution: The distribution of two or more random variables together. It assigns a probability to every combination of their values (the product space) or, for continuous variables, a joint probability density function \(f_{X,Y}(x,y)\) whose integral over a region gives the probability of that region.

  • Kernel density estimate: A smooth alternative to the histogram, calculated from a sample without assuming a model. A small smooth curve (the kernel, by default a Normal density) is placed on top of every observation and these curves are averaged. In R, it is calculated with density().

  • Kurtosis: The central moment for \(k = 4\). It measures how thick the tails of a distribution are, i.e., how much of the distribution is spread far away from the mean.

  • Marginal distribution: The distribution of one random variable in a joint distribution, regardless of the value of the others. It is recovered by adding the joint probabilities over the other variable (discrete) or by integrating the joint density over it (continuous, which gives the marginal density), e.g., \(f_Y(y)=\int f_{X,Y}(x,y)\,dx\).

  • Mean or Expected value: The expectation of the random variable itself:

\[ \mu = \mathbb{E}[X]. \]

It is the probability-weighted average value of a distribution, when it exists. For observed data, the sample mean is the expected value of the empirical distribution, which simplifies to the arithmetic mean:

\[ \bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i. \]

  • Mean squared error (MSE): The expected squared distance between an estimator and the parameter that it estimates. It combines bias and variance into one measure of the quality of an estimator: \[ \operatorname{MSE}(\hat\theta) = \mathbb{E}[(\hat\theta-\theta)^2] = \operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta) + \operatorname{Bias}(\hat\theta)^2. \]

  • Method of moments: A procedure to construct estimators by equating expectations of the model distribution (the mean and central moments) to the same expectations of the empirical distribution of the sample, and solving the resulting equations for the parameters.

  • Model: Mathematical approximation to the data generating process. It involves at least one distribution. Often one or more of the parameters in that distribution vary as a function of covariates or groups, either deterministically or stochastically (with additional distributions).

  • Observation: The value actually recorded in the real world (e.g., the number of seeds that survived in one particular quadrat). It is written with a lower-case letter, \(x\), whereas the random variable is written \(X\).

  • Overdispersion: Variation that is greater than the variation expected under a reference model, usually the Poisson distribution, for which the variance equals the mean. It does not identify the ecological cause: clustering, habitat differences, omitted predictors, detection variation and dependence among observations can all produce it. The opposite case, a variance lower than expected, is called underdispersion.

  • Parameter: A fixed, unknown quantity that defines a model distribution, such as the probability \(p\) of a Binomial distribution or the mean \(\mu\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\) of a Normal distribution.

  • Plug-in estimate: An estimate obtained by replacing an unknown parameter in a formula by its estimate. For example, the standard error of the mean of Poisson counts, \(\sqrt{\lambda/n}\), is estimated by \(\sqrt{\hat\lambda/n}\).

  • Population: The complete set of units, events, or possible outcomes to which a scientific question refers. In modelling, it may be a finite set of real organisms or sites, or a conceptual population described by a probability distribution. This is not always a useful concept, instead we will focus on the concept of data generating process.

  • Probability density function: The function \(f_X(x)\) that defines the distribution of a continuous random variable. The probability of an interval is the integral of the density over that interval, \(P(a<X\leq b)=\int_a^b f_X(x)\,dx\), so the density is never negative and its total area is one. A density is a probability per unit of \(X\): it is not the probability of a single value, it changes with the units of measurement, and it can be larger than one.

  • Probability distribution: A function that assigns probabilities to every possible set of values a random variable. For discrete random variables, it is derived through addition from the probability mass function. For continuous variables, it is derived through integration from the probability density function.

  • Probability mass function: The function \(P(X=x)\) that assigns a probability to every possible value of a discrete random variable. The probabilities of all possible values add up to one, and the probability of any set of values is obtained by adding the individual probabilities.

  • Pseudo-random sequence: A sequence of numbers generated by a deterministic algorithm and designed to have the mathematical properties needed for random sampling. It is what the random samplers in R produce, which makes simulations reproducible.

  • Quantile: A value below which a specified cumulative probability lies. For data, we calculate quantiles on the empirical distribution of the sample.

  • Quantile function: The inverse of the cumulative probability function: given a cumulative probability \(\alpha\) it returns the value \(q_\alpha\) at which that probability is reached. For a discrete variable, \(q_\alpha=\min\{x:F_X(x)\geq\alpha\}\); for a continuous variable, \(F_X(q_\alpha)=\alpha\). The median is the \(0.5\) quantile. In R, it is implemented by the q* functions.

  • Random variable: A numerical quantity that can take any value within a prescribed set (its support). It can be used to model error, variability or uncertainty. Random variables can be discrete or continuous.

  • Relative frequency: The number of times a value appears in a sample divided by the sample size. For independent observations from the same distribution, it converges in probability to the probability mass of that value as the sample size grows.

  • Sample: A collection of values that have been observed or simulated through a data generating process.

  • Sampling distribution: The distribution of the estimates of an estimator across repeated hypothetical experiments with the same design and sample size. It is an imaginary construct: in a real study we carry out the experiment once and never observe the sampling distribution itself, but we can approximate it with a simulation.

  • Seed: The starting state of the algorithm that generates a pseudo-random sequence. If the same seed is set before the same simulation, R produces the same sequence again. In R, it is set with set.seed().

  • Simulation: The use of random sampling from a model with known parameters to generate possible samples, as a way of analysing the statistical properties of a model or of an estimation procedure.

  • Skewness: The central moment for \(k = 3\). It measures whether a distribution is symmetric around its mean.

  • Standard deviation: The square root of a variance. It describes the variation among observations, which is a different quantity from the standard error.

  • Standard error (SE): The standard deviation of the sampling distribution of an estimator, \[ \operatorname{SE}(\hat\theta) = \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta)}. \] It describes the variation of an estimate across hypothetical experiments and has the same units as the estimate. A larger sample reduces the standard error, but it does not reduce the standard deviation of the observations.

  • Support: The set of values that a random variable can take. For continuous distributions, the support can be unconstrained (any real value), positive (any value above zero) or constrained (any value within a range).

  • Variance: A measure of spread around the expected value, the 2nd central moment. For data, we calculate the variance of the empirical distribution of the sample. \[ \operatorname{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[(X-\mathbb{E}[X])^2]. \]

  • Variance of an estimator: The variance of its sampling distribution. For the mean of \(n\) i.i.d. observations it decreases as the sample size grows, whereas the variance of the individual observations does not change.

  • Wald interval: A confidence interval of the form \(\hat\theta \pm z\,\widehat{\operatorname{SE}}(\hat\theta)\), where \(z\) is a quantile of the standard Normal distribution (e.g., \(z \approx 1.96\) for a 95% interval). It relies on the sampling distribution of the estimator being approximately Normal, so its coverage can differ from the confidence level in small samples.

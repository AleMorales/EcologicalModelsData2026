Glossary

  • ADREPORT(): A function of RTMB that is called inside a negative log-likelihood to ask for the standard error of a quantity calculated from the parameters, such as \(k=e^{\log k}\) when the model is fitted on the log scale. The standard error is read from the result of sdreport(). TMB calculates it with the delta method. See also delta method.

  • Akaike information criterion (AIC): A criterion to compare models fitted to the same data, calculated as \[ \mathrm{AIC} = 2K - 2\,\ell(\hat\theta), \] where \(\ell(\hat\theta)\) is the maximum log-likelihood of the model and \(K\) is the number of parameters that were estimated. Among models fitted to the same observations, the one with the lowest AIC is preferred, and only differences in AIC are meaningful. AIC ranks the candidate models: it does not tell us whether the best of them describes the data well. For small samples the corrected AIC adds a penalty that disappears as the number of observations \(n\) grows, \[ \mathrm{AICc}=\mathrm{AIC}+\frac{2K(K+1)}{n-K-1} \] (Chapter 4). For grouped data \(n\) is ambiguous, so grouped models are compared with AIC and not with AICc (Chapter 7).

  • Aleatoric probability: Probability interpreted as the relative frequency at which different values would be observed across all the possible replications of the same experiment or observation protocol. It relates to measurement error and to stochasticity and variability in ecological systems. This is the interpretation of probability used in this course.

  • Asymptote: A line that the graph of a function approaches. A horizontal asymptote is a finite limit of the function as \(x\to\infty\) (or \(-\infty\)). The upper asymptote \(a\) of the Michaelis–Menten function \(ax/(b+x)\) is an example.

  • Asymptotic efficiency: An estimator is asymptotically efficient when, in large samples, its variance is as small as the Cramér–Rao bound allows, so that no other regular estimator is more precise. Maximum likelihood estimators have this property under regularity conditions.

  • Asymptotically normal: An estimator is asymptotically normal when its sampling distribution approaches a Normal distribution as the sample size grows. For the mean of i.i.d. observations from a distribution with finite variance, this follows from the central limit theorem. The maximum likelihood estimator of any parameter is asymptotically normal under regularity conditions, centred on the parameter and with a variance equal to the inverse of the Fisher information.

  • Automatic differentiation: The calculation of the derivatives of a function from the code that computes it, exactly up to rounding error and without a formula written by us. RTMB uses it to supply the gradient of the negative log-likelihood to optim() (the element gr of the object made by MakeADFun()), and the same derivatives are behind sdreport(), ADREPORT() and the Laplace approximation (Chapter 6).

  • Bandwidth: The standard deviation of the kernel used in a kernel density estimate. It plays the same role as the bin width of a histogram: a small bandwidth gives a wiggly estimate and a large bandwidth a very smooth one.

  • Bayesian information criterion (BIC): A criterion to compare models fitted to the same data, calculated as \[ \mathrm{BIC}=K\log(n)-2\,\ell(\hat\theta), \] with the symbols of AIC and \(n\) the number of independent observations. Each parameter costs \(\log(n)\) units instead of two, so for most data sets BIC penalises additional parameters more strongly than AIC, and in large samples it tends to prefer simpler models. It is used like AIC, as a difference between models, although its name comes from its derivation in Bayesian inference (Chapter 6). It is not used for grouped models, for which \(n\) is ambiguous.

  • Bias: The difference between the mean of the sampling distribution of an estimator and the parameter that it estimates, \[ \operatorname{Bias}(\hat\theta) = E[\hat\theta] - \theta. \] An estimator is unbiased when its bias is zero. Bias is a property of the whole sampling distribution: individual estimates of an unbiased estimator still fall above or below the parameter.

  • Central limit theorem: The mean of \(n\) i.i.d. observations from a distribution with finite variance has a sampling distribution that approaches a Normal distribution as \(n\) grows, regardless of the distribution of the individual observations.

  • Central moment: An expectation used to describe features of a distribution. The \(k\)th central moment is \(E[(X-E[X])^k]\). Variance is the central moment for \(k = 2\). Skewness and kurtosis are calculated from the central moments for \(k = 3\) and \(k = 4\).

  • Characteristic scale: The distance along the \(x\) axis over which a function changes by a fixed proportion. It is the value of a scale parameter that divides \(x\), or the inverse of one that multiplies \(x\). For the exponential decay \(ae^{-bx}\) it is \(1/b\), the distance over which the function falls by a factor \(e\).

  • Composition: The application of one function to the output of another, \(f(x)=g(h(x))\), where \(h\) is the inner function and \(g\) the outer function. It is used to give a function the range that a quantity requires, for example \(e^{a+bx}\) for a positive mean or the inverse logit of \(a+bx\) for a probability.

  • Concave and convex: A function is concave where its second derivative is negative, so that the graph bends downwards and the slope decreases as \(x\) increases. It is convex where the second derivative is positive, so that the graph bends upwards and the slope increases. The Michaelis–Menten function is concave for all \(x\ge0\).

  • Conditional distribution: The distribution of one random variable for a given value of another, \[ P(X_1=x_1\mid X_2=x_2)=\frac{P(X_1=x_1,X_2=x_2)}{P(X_2=x_2)}, \] the joint probability divided by the marginal probability of the given value (for continuous variables, \(f(x\mid y)=f(x,y)/f(y)\) with densities). Two random variables are independent when the conditional distribution of each equals its marginal distribution. Grouped models are written with conditional distributions: the observations of a group for a given value of its random effect (Chapter 7).

  • Confidence band: The confidence intervals of a fitted curve at every value of the predictor, drawn together. The intervals are calculated with ADREPORT() on a scale on which the curve is unbounded (e.g. the logit of a probability) and their endpoints are transformed back. A band is pointwise: at each value of \(x\), its interval contains the value of the curve under the data generating process in approximately 95% of hypothetical samples; a band for the whole curve at once would be wider. It describes the uncertainty of the curve, not where new observations fall (Chapter 6).

  • Confidence interval: A range of values for a parameter, calculated from a sample with a procedure that has a known behaviour across repeated hypothetical samples. Once an interval has been calculated, it either contains the parameter or it does not, so the confidence level is a property of the procedure and not a probability that the parameter lies in that particular interval.

  • Confidence level: The fraction of repeated hypothetical samples for which a confidence interval calculated with a given procedure would contain the parameter (e.g., 95%). The actual fraction, the coverage, may differ from the intended level when the procedure relies on approximations.

  • Consistency: An estimator \(\hat\theta_n\) calculated from a sample of size \(n\) is consistent if it converges in probability to the parameter \(\theta\), that is, for any positive margin \(\varepsilon\), \[ \lim_{n \to \infty} P\left(\left|\hat\theta_n - \theta\right| \ge \varepsilon\right)=0. \] It describes the probability of large errors, not the ordering of the estimates of two particular samples.

  • Continuous random variable: A random variable that can take any value within an interval of the real numbers (e.g., all positive numbers, or all numbers between 0 and 1). Its values cannot be listed one by one, the probability of any single value is zero, and its distribution assigns probabilities to intervals through a probability density function.

  • Correlation: A parameter of a joint distribution, such as the bivariate Normal distribution, that controls how strongly two random variables change together. For a bivariate Normal distribution, a correlation of zero means that the two variables are independent.

  • Coverage: The fraction of repeated hypothetical samples for which the confidence interval calculated with a given procedure contains the parameter. It can be checked by simulation when the parameter that generated the samples is known.

  • Cramér–Rao bound: A lower limit for the variance of an unbiased estimator \(\tilde\theta\): it cannot be smaller than the inverse of the Fisher information, \(\operatorname{Var}(\tilde\theta) \ge 1/\mathcal{I}(\theta)\). The information in a sample therefore sets a limit to the precision with which a parameter can be estimated from it, whatever the method of estimation.

  • Critical point: A value of \(x\) at which the derivative of a function is zero, so that the function is flat there. It is a maximum when the slope changes from positive to negative, a minimum when it changes from negative to positive, and a saddle point when the sign of the slope does not change.

  • Cross-validation: An estimate of how well a model predicts observations that were not used to fit it. The observations are split into parts; the model is fitted to all but one part and scored on the held-out part, until every part has been held out once. In leave-one-out cross-validation each part is one observation: every parameter is estimated again from the other \(n-1\) observations, and the held-out observation is scored by its log score, the log probability (or log density) that the refitted model assigns to it. The score of the model is the sum of the log scores, and a larger (less negative) score is better. It needs no penalty for the number of parameters (Chapter 6). See also leave-one-group-out cross-validation.

  • Cumulative probability function: The function \(F_X(x)=P(X \le x)\), the probability that a random variable takes a value at or below \(x\). It is calculated by adding probability masses (discrete) or by integrating the density (continuous), it is always between zero and one, and it never decreases as \(x\) increases. In R, it is implemented by the p* functions.

  • Data-generating process: The mechanism that produces observations in an experiment, survey or any other relevant research protocol. It includes ecological or physical processes, systematic relationships among variables, variation among units, measurement and sampling procedures, and observational error. A statistical model is a simplified representation of this process.

  • Delta method: An approximation of the standard error of a quantity \(g(\hat\theta)\) that is calculated from an estimate \(\hat\theta\), \[ \operatorname{SE}\big(g(\hat\theta)\big) \approx \left|g'(\hat\theta)\right|\,\operatorname{SE}(\hat\theta), \] where \(g'\) is the derivative of \(g\). In plain English, the uncertainty of the estimate is multiplied by the slope of the function that links it to the new quantity. With several parameters, the partial derivatives of \(g\) are combined with the covariances of the estimates. The approximation is better when the sample is large and the function is close to a straight line over the range of the estimate. RTMB applies it to the quantities requested with ADREPORT() from Chapter 4 onward, where a box gives the formula, and Chapter 6 applies it to a fitted curve.

  • Density-scaled histogram: A histogram in which the height of each bar is the relative frequency of the bin divided by the width of the bin. The area of each bar equals the relative frequency and the areas of all bars add up to one, so the histogram can be compared with a probability density function.

  • Derivative: The slope of a function at a point, \(f'(x)=df/dx\): the change in the output per unit change in the input, measured over an interval so short that the curve is indistinguishable from a straight line. Its units are the units of the output divided by those of the input. The derivative of a log-likelihood is zero at its maximum.

  • Deterministic function: A function that assigns one output to each input for given values of its parameters, so that the same input always gives the same output. In a model for data it is the part that describes how the expected response changes with a predictor.

  • Discrete random variable: A random variable that takes values from a finite or countably infinite set, such as the counts \(0, 1, 2, \ldots\). A set is finite when it has a fixed maximum number of values (e.g., 0 to 4 surviving seeds out of four) and countably infinite when there is no pre-specified maximum but the values can still be listed (e.g., any non-negative integer).

  • Dispersion ratio: The ratio of the sample variance to the sample mean. For independent counts from a Poisson distribution it tends to be close to one, so a substantially larger ratio suggests overdispersion. It is an exploratory diagnostic and does not identify the cause of the extra variation.

  • Dispersion statistic: The summary of spread that DHARMa calculates from simulations of a fitted model: the variance of the observed values around their expected values, divided by the average of the same variance in the simulated samples. It is close to one when the model allows the right amount of variation, and above one when the observations vary more than the model allows. Like the dispersion ratio, it is exploratory evidence (Chapter 6).

  • Domain: The set of values of the input \(x\) for which a function is used. Many ecological functions are used only for \(x\ge0\) (a density, a size, a time).

  • Empirical cumulative distribution: The cumulative function of the empirical distribution: the fraction of observations at or below each value. It requires neither bins nor a bandwidth and, for continuous data, it increases by \(1/n\) at each observed value. In R, it is calculated with ecdf().

  • Empirical distribution: The distribution represented directly by observed data, by assuming each possible observation has a probability mass equal to its relative frequency in the sample. It describes the observed sample in a way that we can compute quantiles and moments, it is not a description of the data generating process.

  • Epistemic probability: Probability interpreted as how certain we are about each possible value when predicting an outcome, which depends on the knowledge or information that we have about the system.

  • Estimate: The value that an estimator returns for the sample that we actually observed. It is marked with a hat, e.g., \(\hat\lambda = 4\).

  • Estimator: A rule (i.e., a function) that we apply to a sample to calculate a value for a parameter. Since we treat the observations as random variables, an estimator is also a random variable: a different sample would give a different estimate.

  • Expectation: An operator that maps a function of a random variable to its probability-weighted average under a specified distribution. For a function \(f(X)\),

\[ E[f(X)] = \begin{cases} \sum_x f(x)\,P(X=x), & \text{for a discrete random variable},\\ \int f(x)f_X(x)\,dx, & \text{for a continuous random variable}, \end{cases} \]

  • Finite difference: An approximation of a derivative: the change in a function between two points very close to each other, divided by the distance between them. It needs no calculus, and software uses it when no formula for the derivative is available (Chapter 5).

  • Fisher information: A measure of how much information a sample contains about a parameter, written \(\mathcal{I}(\theta)\). The variance of a maximum likelihood estimator approaches its inverse in large samples. From a sample we calculate the observed information, minus the second derivative of the log-likelihood at the estimate, \(\hat{\mathcal{I}} = -\ell''(\hat\theta)\). A sharply curved log-likelihood has a large information and a small variance of the estimator. With several parameters the information is a matrix: the Hessian of the negative log-likelihood at the estimates.

  • Fixed effect: A parameter of a grouped model that is shared by all the groups, such as the rate of a response curve or the effect of a group-level predictor (origin, treatment). It is estimated as an ordinary parameter. See also random effect and mixed-effects model.

  • Four faces of a distribution: The four functions that every distribution has: the probability mass (or density), the cumulative probability, the quantile function and the random sampler. In R they are implemented by functions with the prefixes d, p, q and r, followed by a short name for the distribution (e.g., dbinom, pbinom, qbinom and rbinom).

  • Glivenko–Cantelli theorem: The empirical cumulative distribution of an i.i.d. sample converges uniformly (that is, for all values at once) to the cumulative distribution of the model as the sample size grows. It extends the law of large numbers, which applies to the relative frequency of one event at a time.

  • Gradient: The vector of the partial derivatives of a function with respect to each of its parameters, that is, its slope in the direction of each parameter. Optimisers such as BFGS use the gradient of the negative log-likelihood to choose their steps; RTMB calculates it by automatic differentiation (Chapter 6).

  • Group-level predictor: A predictor measured on a group and not on each observation (the origin of a plant, the treatment that it received, the habitat of a site), so that all the observations of a group share its value. In a mixed model it enters as a fixed effect on the parameter that varies among groups, and the random effects then describe the variation that remains among groups with the same values of the predictors (Chapter 7).

  • Half-life: The time \(t_{1/2}\) over which an exponential decay \(ae^{-bt}\) falls to half of its value, \(t_{1/2}=\log 2/b\), wherever we start. It is the half-maximum idea applied to a decline, and it is read on the time axis.

  • Half-maximum: The value of \(x\) at which a saturating function reaches half of its asymptote. For the Michaelis–Menten function \(ax/(b+x)\) it is \(b\). It is read on the horizontal axis and has the units of \(x\), unlike half of the asymptote, \(a/2\), which is a value of the response.

  • Hessian: The matrix of second derivatives of a function with respect to every pair of its arguments. With hessian = TRUE, optim() returns the Hessian of the negative log-likelihood at the estimates, on the scale of the parameters that were optimised. This is the observed information matrix, and its inverse (calculated with solve()) approximates the variances and covariances of the estimates. The function sdreport() of RTMB calculates the Hessian itself and returns the standard errors and the covariance matrix.

  • Histogram: A summary of a sample of continuous measurements. The range of the measurements is divided into intervals (called bins) and the observations in each bin are counted. Dividing each count by the sample size gives the relative frequency of the bin. The width of the bins is a choice that changes how the same sample looks.

  • Hypothetical sample: Another sample that the data generating process could have produced, with the same design and sample size as the data. Because the data are treated as a random sample from the model, hypothetical samples are drawn from the model. What counts as one sample is set by the data that are analysed: several field campaigns analysed together form one sample. Many statistics textbooks call a hypothetical sample an experiment; this course does not, because much ecological data comes from observational studies.

  • Independence: Two random variables are independent if knowledge about one of them does not change the probabilities for the other. Equivalently, their joint distribution is the product of their marginal distributions, \(P(X_1=x_1, X_2=x_2)=P(X_1=x_1)P(X_2=x_2)\) for discrete variables and \(f_{X,Y}(x,y)=f_X(x)f_Y(y)\) for continuous variables. It is a modelling assumption: it is not guaranteed merely because observations were collected at different locations or times.

  • Independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.): A set of random variables are i.i.d. when each of them follows the same distribution and the value of one does not change the probabilities for the others.

  • Inflection point: A value of \(x\) at which the second derivative of a function changes sign, so that the graph changes from convex to concave or the reverse. The slope is steepest (or shallowest) there. The midpoint \(c\) of the logistic function is its inflection point.

  • Interaction: Two predictors interact when the effect of one depends on the value of the other. In a model with a baseline plus effects, the interaction is the difference between the effect of one predictor at the two levels of the other, e.g. the effect of chilling in plants from Mississippi minus its effect in plants from Quebec (Chapter 7).

  • Invariance: The maximum likelihood estimate of a function of a parameter is that function of the maximum likelihood estimate, \(\widehat{g(\theta)} = g(\hat\theta)\). It allows us to fit a model on a transformed scale (e.g., the logarithm of a positive parameter) and to report the estimates on the original scale. Invariance carries over the estimate but not unbiasedness.

  • Jensen’s inequality: \(E[g(X)]\ge g(E[X])\) if \(g\) is a convex function and \(E[g(X)]\le g(E[X])\) if \(g\) is concave, so the curvature decides the sign of the difference. In plain English, the mean of a transformed variable is not the transformation of the mean, and this holds in general for non-linear functions. A variance is a kind of mean and a standard error is its square root, so a standard error cannot be back-transformed by applying the function to it. See also delta method.

  • Joint confidence region: A region of the parameter space calculated so that, across repeated hypothetical samples, it contains the combination of parameter values that generated the data with a given frequency. For two parameters, an approximate 95% region contains the combinations whose relative log-likelihood is above \(-3.00\) (half the 0.95 quantile of a chi-squared distribution with two degrees of freedom). It extends beyond the likelihood profile interval of each parameter, because capturing two parameters at once is more demanding than capturing one.

  • Joint distribution: The distribution of two or more random variables together. It assigns a probability to every combination of their values (the product space) or, for continuous variables, a joint probability density function \(f_{X,Y}(x,y)\) whose integral over a region gives the probability of that region.

  • Kernel density estimate: A smooth alternative to the histogram, calculated from a sample without assuming a model. A small smooth curve (the kernel, by default a Normal density) is placed on top of every observation and these curves are averaged. In R, it is calculated with density().

  • Kullback–Leibler divergence: A measure of how far a model distribution is from the distribution \(g\) of the data generating process. For a discrete variable, \[ D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\theta) = \sum_x g(x)\log\frac{g(x)}{P(X=x\mid\theta)}. \] It is zero when the model assigns the right probability to every value and positive otherwise. Maximum likelihood amounts to minimising it, so the estimator of a misspecified model converges to the parameter values that bring the model closest to the data generating process.

  • Kurtosis: The fourth central moment divided by \(\sigma^4\), where \(\sigma\) is the standard deviation, so it has no units. It measures how thick the tails of a distribution are, i.e., how much of the distribution is spread far away from the mean. It is often reported as the excess kurtosis, which subtracts 3, the kurtosis of a Normal distribution.

  • Laplace approximation: An approximation of the integral of a function with a single peak. Near the peak the logarithm of the function is close to a parabola, so the function is close to a Normal-shaped curve, whose area depends only on the height of the peak and the curvature of the logarithm there. RTMB uses it, through the argument random of MakeADFun(), to integrate random effects out of the likelihood. It is accurate when each group has many observations with nearly Normal conditional distributions, exact for Normal linear mixed models, and less accurate for few observations per group, small counts or binary data (Chapter 7).

  • Law of large numbers: As the sample size \(n\) of i.i.d. observations grows, the mean of the sample (and so a relative frequency, which is the mean of indicators) gets close to its expected value with a probability that approaches one. In its weak form: for any positive margin \(\varepsilon\), the probability that the sample mean differs from the expected value by more than \(\varepsilon\) tends to zero (Chapter 3). See also consistency.

  • Leave-one-group-out cross-validation: Cross-validation for grouped data in which one whole group is held out at a time: every parameter is estimated again from the other groups, and the held-out group is scored by its marginal log-likelihood under that fit. The score is the sum over groups. Holding out single observations would leave the other observations of the same group in the fit (Chapter 7).

  • Likelihood function: The joint probability (or, for continuous data, the joint density) of the observed sample, read as a function of the parameters with the data held fixed. For i.i.d. observations, \[ L(\theta\mid x_1,\ldots,x_n) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(X_i=x_i\mid\theta). \] It is not a probability distribution for the parameter: it does not add up or integrate to one over the parameter values. We use it to compare parameter values, and the value that the data support best is the maximum likelihood estimate.

  • Likelihood profile: The maximum log-likelihood reached for each value of a parameter of interest, when the other parameters are re-estimated for every value, \[ \ell_{\mathrm{prof}}(\theta_1) = \max_{\theta_2}\,\ell(\theta_1,\theta_2). \] In this course the re-estimation is done with optim(), one value at a time or with TMB::tmbprofile(). A profile does not depend on how the other parameters are written, whereas a slice does.

  • Likelihood profile interval: An approximate confidence interval that contains all the values of a parameter whose relative profile log-likelihood is above \(-1.92\) (half the 0.95 quantile of a chi-squared distribution with one degree of freedom). It follows from Wilks’ theorem. It does not depend on the scale of the parameter and it follows the asymmetry of the likelihood, which is why it is the reference method of this book. Its endpoints are found with confint() applied to the result of TMB::tmbprofile(). For a model with one parameter it is simply called the likelihood interval.

  • Likelihood slice: The log-likelihood as a function of one parameter, with the other parameters fixed at one value, normally their estimates. A slice needs no optimisation, but it treats the other parameters as known, so it is narrower than the likelihood profile unless the estimates of the parameters do not depend on each other.

  • Limit: The value that a function approaches as its input approaches a given value, written \(\lim_{x\to x_0}f(x)\), or as \(x\to\infty\) for the end behaviour. A limit can exist even where the function itself is not defined.

  • Location parameter: A parameter that shifts a function along the \(x\) axis without changing its shape, as \(c\) does when \(x\) is replaced by \(x-c\). It has the units of \(x\).

  • Log-likelihood: The natural logarithm of the likelihood. For i.i.d. observations it is a sum of log probabilities (or log densities), \[ \ell(\theta\mid x_1,\ldots,x_n) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\log P(X_i=x_i\mid\theta), \] which in R is calculated with the argument log = TRUE of the d* functions. It has its maximum at the same parameter value as the likelihood, and it avoids numbers that are too small for the computer to represent. Its value has no meaning by itself: only differences between log-likelihoods calculated from the same data are meaningful.

  • Logit: The transformation \(\operatorname{logit}(p)=\log\left(p/(1-p)\right)\), which maps a probability between zero and one onto all the real numbers. Its inverse, the inverse logit \(\operatorname{logit}^{-1}(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})\), turns any real number into a value between zero and one. In R, qlogis() calculates the logit and plogis() its inverse. The course optimises probabilities on the logit scale so that they stay between zero and one (Chapter 4).

  • MakeADFun(): The function of RTMB that processes a negative log-likelihood written with two named lists, one for the parameters and one for the data, so that the tools of the course can work with it. It returns an object with the starting values (par), the value of the negative log-likelihood (fn) and its slope (gr), which optim() uses, and with the function simulate(). The same object is the argument of sdreport() and of TMB::tmbprofile(). Chapter 4 uses it from the first fit and Chapter 6 says more about it. In Chapter 7 the argument random names the random effects that it integrates out with the Laplace approximation, and map fixes parameters at given values.

  • Marginal distribution: The distribution of one random variable in a joint distribution, regardless of the value of the others. It is recovered by adding the joint probabilities over the other variable (discrete) or by integrating the joint density over it (continuous, which gives the marginal density), e.g., \(f_Y(y)=\int f_{X,Y}(x,y)\,dx\).

  • Marginal likelihood: The likelihood of the parameters of a model with random effects (fixed effects and variance components), in which the random effects, which are not observed, have been integrated out. For group \(j\), \(L_j(\boldsymbol\theta)=\int f(\mathbf y_j\mid u)\,f(u\mid\sigma_{\mathrm{group}})\,du\), the likelihood of the observations of the group averaged over the values that its random effect could take; the marginal likelihood is the product over the groups. Its maximisation is called maximum marginal likelihood (Chapter 7).

  • Maximum likelihood estimator (MLE): The value of a parameter for which the likelihood of the observed sample is highest, \(\hat\theta = \arg\max_\theta L(\theta\mid x_1,\ldots,x_n)\). The abbreviation is used for the rule and for the value that it returns for a sample. Under regularity conditions, MLEs are consistent, asymptotically normal and asymptotically efficient. In finite samples they are in general biased.

  • Mean or Expected value: The expectation of the random variable itself:

\[ \mu = E[X]. \]

It is the probability-weighted average value of a distribution, when it exists. For observed data, the sample mean is the expected value of the empirical distribution, which simplifies to the arithmetic mean:

\[ \bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i. \]

  • Mean squared error (MSE): The expected squared distance between an estimator and the parameter that it estimates. It combines bias and variance into one measure of the quality of an estimator: \[ \operatorname{MSE}(\hat\theta) = E[(\hat\theta-\theta)^2] = \operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta) + \operatorname{Bias}(\hat\theta)^2. \]

  • Mechanistic function: A function derived from assumptions about the process that produces a pattern, so that its parameters measure something in the system, such as a rate or a handling time. See also phenomenological function.

  • Method of moments: A procedure to construct estimators by equating expectations of the model distribution (the mean and central moments) to the same expectations of the empirical distribution of the sample, and solving the resulting equations for the parameters.

  • Misspecified model: A model that does not contain the distribution of the data generating process, whatever the values of its parameters. The maximum likelihood estimator then converges to the parameter values that minimise the Kullback–Leibler divergence, and standard errors and intervals calculated as if the model had generated the data are approximations.

  • Mixed-effects model (or mixed model): A model with both fixed effects and random effects. Because it describes the data in levels (the groups, then the observations within a group), it is also called a hierarchical or multilevel model (Chapter 7).

  • Model: Mathematical approximation to the data generating process. It involves at least one distribution. Often one or more of the parameters in that distribution vary as a function of covariates or groups, either deterministically or stochastically (with additional distributions).

  • Monotonic function: A function that only increases or only decreases over its domain, so that its derivative never changes sign. Exponential decay and the Michaelis–Menten function are monotonic; the Ricker function is not.

  • Negative log-likelihood (NLL): Minus the log-likelihood, \(-\ell(\theta)\). The function optim() minimises by default, so we give it the NLL: the parameter value with the lowest NLL is the one with the highest likelihood. From Chapter 4 onward the NLL is written as a function of two named lists, the parameters and the data, so that RTMB can process it with MakeADFun().

  • Nested functions: A function is nested in another when it is the special case obtained by fixing one of the parameters of the other at a particular value. The Michaelis–Menten function is nested in the Hassell function, which has a third parameter \(c\), and is recovered when \(c=1\). Nested functions are the usual candidates in a comparison of models.

  • OBS(): A function of RTMB that is called inside a negative log-likelihood to mark a data variable as an observation. It makes it possible to simulate the data: the function simulate() of the object made by MakeADFun() replaces every marked variable with values drawn from the model, which gives one hypothetical sample.

  • Observation: The value actually recorded in the real world (e.g., the number of seeds that survived in one particular quadrat). It is written with a lower-case letter, \(x\), whereas the random variable is written \(X\).

  • Optimiser: An algorithm that searches for the parameter values at which a function reaches its minimum. It starts from values that we provide and moves step by step towards lower values until it cannot improve any further. The course minimises the negative log-likelihood with optim() and the method BFGS; a convergence code of zero says that the optimiser stopped, not that it found the maximum of the likelihood (Chapter 4, Chapter 8).

  • Overdispersion: Variation that is greater than the variation expected under a reference model, usually the Poisson distribution, for which the variance equals the mean. It does not identify the ecological cause: clustering, habitat differences, omitted predictors, detection variation and dependence among observations can all produce it. The opposite case, a variance lower than expected, is called underdispersion.

  • Parameter: A fixed, unknown quantity that defines a model distribution, such as the probability \(p\) of a Binomial distribution or the mean \(\mu\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\) of a Normal distribution. In a deterministic function, a parameter is a constant of the formula that stays fixed while the input varies, such as the asymptote of a Michaelis–Menten function.

  • Partial derivative: The derivative of a function of several variables or parameters with respect to one of them, taken with the others held fixed, written \(\partial f/\partial a\). The partial derivative of a function with respect to a parameter tells us how much the function moves, at a given \(x\), when that parameter changes slightly.

  • Partial pooling: A way of treating a parameter that may differ among groups: each group has its own value, and the values are drawn from a distribution whose variance is estimated, so that all the groups inform the distribution and the distribution informs the value of each group. It lies between complete pooling (one value shared by all the groups) and no pooling (a separate, unconnected parameter for each group). See also random effect and shrinkage (Chapter 7).

  • Phenomenological function: A function chosen because it has the shape of the pattern in the data, so that its parameters describe that shape (a height, a location, a steepness) and not a process. See also mechanistic function.

  • Piecewise function: A function defined by different formulas on different parts of its domain, such as the threshold and hockey-stick functions. In R it is written with ifelse() (Chapter 5).

  • Plug-in estimate: An estimate obtained by replacing an unknown parameter in a formula by its estimate. For example, the standard error of the mean of Poisson counts, \(\sqrt{\lambda/n}\), is estimated by \(\sqrt{\hat\lambda/n}\).

  • Population: The complete set of units, events, or possible outcomes to which a scientific question refers. In modelling, it may be a finite set of real organisms or sites, or a conceptual population described by a probability distribution. This is not always a useful concept, instead we will focus on the concept of data generating process.

  • Precision (of the Beta-Binomial distribution): The parameter \(\kappa=a+b\), where \(a\) and \(b\) are the shape parameters of the Beta distribution of the probability of each sampling unit. With the mean probability \(p\), \(a=\kappa p\) and \(b=\kappa(1-p)\). A large precision means little variation in the probability among units, and as \(\kappa\) grows the Beta-Binomial approaches the Binomial distribution (Chapter 6).

  • Predicted group effect: The value of the random effect of a group that RTMB returns (summary(sdreport(...), "random")): the peak of the integrand of the marginal likelihood of the group, that is, the most probable value of its random effect for its observations and the estimated parameters (also called the conditional mode). It is a prediction, not an estimate of a parameter, and it is shrunk towards the typical value of groups with the same predictors (Chapter 7).

  • Probability density function: The function \(f_X(x)\) that defines the distribution of a continuous random variable. The probability of an interval is the integral of the density over that interval, \(P(a<X\leq b)=\int_a^b f_X(x)\,dx\), so the density is never negative and its total area is one. A density is a probability per unit of \(X\): it is not the probability of a single value, it changes with the units of measurement, and it can be larger than one.

  • Probability distribution: A function that assigns probabilities to every possible set of values a random variable. For discrete random variables, it is derived through addition from the probability mass function. For continuous variables, it is derived through integration from the probability density function.

  • Probability mass function: The function \(P(X=x)\) that assigns a probability to every possible value of a discrete random variable. The probabilities of all possible values add up to one, and the probability of any set of values is obtained by adding the individual probabilities.

  • Pseudo-random sequence: A sequence of numbers generated by a deterministic algorithm and designed to have the mathematical properties needed for random sampling. It is what the random samplers in R produce, which makes simulations reproducible.

  • Quadratic approximation: The parabola that has the same value, slope and curvature as a function at a chosen point \(x_0\), \(f(x)\approx f(x_0)+f'(x_0)(x-x_0)+\tfrac12 f''(x_0)(x-x_0)^2\). The Wald interval of Chapter 4 is based on the quadratic approximation of the log-likelihood at its maximum.

  • Quantile: A value below which a specified cumulative probability lies. For data, we calculate quantiles on the empirical distribution of the sample.

  • Quantile function: The inverse of the cumulative probability function: given a cumulative probability \(\alpha\) it returns the value \(q_\alpha\) at which that probability is reached. For a discrete variable, \(q_\alpha=\min\{x:F_X(x)\geq\alpha\}\); for a continuous variable, \(F_X(q_\alpha)=\alpha\). The median is the \(0.5\) quantile. In R, it is implemented by the q* functions.

  • Random effect: The deviation of a group (a plant, a nest, a site) from the value shared by all the groups, described by a distribution, usually Normal with mean zero on a transformed scale, e.g. \(u_j\sim\operatorname{Normal}(0,\sigma^2_{\mathrm{group}})\) on the logarithm of a positive parameter. The random effects are not parameters: they are integrated out of the likelihood, and the values that RTMB returns for them are predicted group effects. “Random” does not mean that the groups were allocated at random (Chapter 7).

  • Random variable: A numerical quantity that can take any value within a prescribed set (its support). It can be used to model error, variability or uncertainty. Random variables can be discrete or continuous.

  • Range: The set of values that the output \(y\) of a function can take. Composition with \(e^{x}\) gives a range of positive values, and with the inverse logit a range between 0 and 1.

  • Relative frequency: The number of times a value appears in a sample divided by the sample size. For independent observations from the same distribution, it converges in probability to the probability mass of that value as the sample size grows.

  • Relative log-likelihood: The difference between the log-likelihood and its maximum, \(\ell(\theta)-\ell(\hat\theta)\). It is zero at the estimate and negative everywhere else, and it shows that only differences in log-likelihood are meaningful.

  • Reparameterisation: Writing the same function or distribution with a different set of parameters, each of which is an expression of the old ones. The curve does not change; the meaning, the units and the correlation of the parameters do. The Michaelis–Menten function and the Holling type II functional response are two parameterisations of one curve.

  • Response curve: A deterministic function that gives one parameter of the distribution of each observation (usually its mean) as a function of a predictor, e.g. the probability that a tadpole is killed as a function of the initial density of tadpoles. The curve and the probability distribution of the observations around it form the model that is fitted by maximum likelihood (Chapter 6).

  • Restricted maximum likelihood (REML): A method of estimating variance components from a likelihood in which the fixed effects have also been integrated out, as if they were random effects with a flat distribution. It reduces the downward bias of maximum likelihood estimates of variances when fixed effects are estimated; for i.i.d. Normal observations it gives the variance estimator that divides by \(n-1\). In RTMB the fixed effects are added to the argument random. For non-linear or non-Normal models it is an approximation, and models with different fixed effects are compared with maximum likelihood fits (Chapter 7).

  • RTMB: An R package in which we write a negative log-likelihood as an R function and that provides the tools to fit it. Its functions for probability mass and density (together with those of the package RTMBdist, which adds more distributions) are used from Chapter 2 onward, and Chapter 4 uses MakeADFun(), sdreport(), ADREPORT() and OBS() to fit models and to calculate their uncertainty. It is built on the package TMB, which is installed with it.

  • Sample: A collection of values that have been observed or simulated through a data generating process.

  • Sampling distribution: The distribution of the estimates of an estimator across repeated hypothetical samples with the same design and sample size. It is an imaginary construct: in a real study we observe one realisation of the data generating process and never observe the sampling distribution itself, but we can approximate it with a simulation.

  • Saturating function: A function that increases and levels off at an upper limit (an asymptote), as the Michaelis–Menten function does.

  • Scale parameter: A parameter that multiplies or divides \(x\) wherever \(x\) appears in a formula. Changing it stretches or compresses the curve along the \(x\) axis without changing its shape.

  • Scaled quantile residual: The fraction of the values simulated from a fitted model for an observation that are smaller than the observed value (for discrete data, drawn at random between the fractions that are smaller and smaller or equal). If the model describes the data generating process, the residuals are approximately uniform between 0 and 1, whatever the distribution of the observations, so they can be read in the same way for any observation model. The course calculates them with the package DHARMa from simulations made with obj$simulate() (Chapter 6).

  • Score: The derivative of the log-likelihood with respect to a parameter. The MLE is the value of the parameter (or parameters) at which the score is zero (Chapter 4). Not to be confused with the log score of cross-validation.

  • sdreport(): A function of RTMB that, applied to an object made by MakeADFun() after a fit, calculates the Hessian of the negative log-likelihood at the estimates and returns their standard errors and covariance matrix, on the scale that was optimised. It also returns the estimates and standard errors of the quantities requested with ADREPORT().

  • Seed: The starting state of the algorithm that generates a pseudo-random sequence. If the same seed is set before the same simulation, R produces the same sequence again. In R, it is set with set.seed().

  • Shrinkage: The pull of the predicted effect of a group towards the typical value of groups with the same predictors, compared with the estimate from the observations of that group alone. It is stronger for groups with few or noisy observations and when the variance among groups is small. It follows from the distribution of the random effects and is a form of regularisation (Chapter 7).

  • Sigmoid function: A function that increases slowly at first, then fast, then slowly again, so that its graph is S-shaped, as the logistic function does.

  • Simulation: The use of random sampling from a model with known parameters to generate possible samples, as a way of analysing the statistical properties of a model or of an estimation procedure.

  • Skewness: The third central moment divided by \(\sigma^3\), where \(\sigma\) is the standard deviation, so it has no units. A distribution that is symmetric around its mean has a skewness of zero.

  • Standard deviation: The square root of a variance. It describes the variation among observations, which is a different quantity from the standard error.

  • Standard error (SE): The standard deviation of the sampling distribution of an estimator, \[ \operatorname{SE}(\hat\theta) = \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta)}. \] It describes the variation of an estimate across hypothetical samples and has the same units as the estimate. A larger sample reduces the standard error, but it does not reduce the standard deviation of the observations.

  • Support: The set of values that a random variable can take. For continuous distributions, the support can be unconstrained (any real value), positive (any value above zero) or constrained (any value within a range).

  • TMB::tmbprofile(): A function of the package TMB (written with its prefix, because RTMB does not export it) that calculates the likelihood profile of one parameter of an object made by MakeADFun(): for each value of the parameter it re-estimates the others. It returns a data frame of values of the parameter and of the negative log-likelihood, and confint() of the result gives the endpoints of the likelihood profile interval.

  • Unimodal function: A function that rises to a single maximum and declines after it (a hump-shaped function), as the Ricker function does.

  • Variance: A measure of spread around the expected value, the 2nd central moment. For data, we calculate the variance of the empirical distribution of the sample. \[ \operatorname{Var}(X) = E[(X-E[X])^2]. \] The variance of a sample, mean((x - mean(x))^2), divides by \(n\); var(x) divides by \(n-1\) (Bessel’s correction), which removes the bias of the estimate of the variance of the model (Chapter 3).

  • Variance component: A variance parameter of a grouped model that describes one level of variation: \(\sigma^2_{\mathrm{group}}\) the variation among groups (the variance of the random effects) and \(\sigma^2_{\mathrm{obs}}\) the variation of the observations around the curve of their group. Each has the scale and the units of the quantity that it describes; the negative log-likelihood usually takes their square roots, the standard deviations (Chapter 7).

  • Variance of an estimator: The variance of its sampling distribution. For the mean of \(n\) i.i.d. observations it decreases as the sample size grows, whereas the variance of the individual observations does not change.

  • Wald interval: A confidence interval of the form \(\hat\theta \pm z\,\widehat{\operatorname{SE}}(\hat\theta)\), where \(z\) is a quantile of the standard Normal distribution (e.g., \(z \approx 1.96\) for a 95% interval). It relies on the sampling distribution of the estimator being approximately Normal, so its coverage can differ from the confidence level in small samples. With maximum likelihood it is a quadratic approximation of the log-likelihood around the estimate, with the standard error obtained from the Hessian (sdreport() calculates it). The interval depends on the scale on which it is calculated, so for a positive parameter it is calculated on the log scale and its endpoints are transformed back. A quantity calculated from the parameters has its own standard error, which ADREPORT() provides through the delta method.

  • Wilks’ theorem: In large samples, twice the difference between the maximum log-likelihood and the profile log-likelihood at the parameter value that generated the data approximately follows a chi-squared distribution, with as many degrees of freedom as the number of parameters that are fixed. It is the basis of likelihood profile intervals and of joint confidence regions.

  • Zero-inflated distribution: A mixture of two processes: with probability \(p_0\) the observation is zero (e.g., a quadrat in which no seeds arrive), and otherwise it follows another distribution (e.g., a Poisson distribution), which can also produce zeros. For a Poisson distribution, \(P(X=0)=p_0+(1-p_0)e^{-\lambda}\), and \(P(X=x)=(1-p_0)e^{-\lambda}\lambda^x/x!\) for \(x>0\). See How to make zero-inflated distributions.

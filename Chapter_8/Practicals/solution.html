Chapter 8: Numerical optimisation — solutions

Published

November 4, 2026

The fully worked first exercise is on the student practical page. These solutions use the same data, tasks and seeds as Exercises 2 and 3 on that page. All three exercises are the core of the practical, and each follows the four steps of a diagnosis: the symptom, the diagnosis, the remedy and the confirmation.

As on that page, we load RTMB first and RTMBdist second, and define the helper cmb() that connects a likelihood to a data set:

# Load RTMB first and RTMBdist second.
library(RTMB)
library(RTMBdist)

# Connect a likelihood to a data set.
cmb = function(f, d) function(p) f(p, d)

1 Exercise 2: Body mass of Adelie penguins

The data set penguins of the package datasets (R 4.5.0 and later) contains the body masses, in grams, of adult penguins of three species near Palmer Station, Antarctica. We fit a Gamma model with shape \(k\) and rate \(r\), the model of the tree volumes in Exercise 4 of the Chapter 4 practical, to the body masses of the 151 Adelie penguins whose mass was recorded,

\[ X_i \sim \operatorname{Gamma}(k, r), \qquad E[X_i]=\frac{k}{r}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X_i)=\frac{k}{r^2}, \]

where \(X_i\) is the body mass of penguin \(i\), with \(\log(k)\) and \(\log(r)\) as the parameters. Started at the method-of-moments estimates, the fit to the masses in grams stops after a single step and reports convergence code 0. Load the data with data(penguins, package = "datasets"), keep the Adelie penguins and remove the one missing mass explicitly.

  1. Fit the Gamma model to the masses in grams with dgamma(), starting at the method-of-moments estimates \(k=\bar x^2/s^2\) and \(r=\bar x/s^2\), where \(\bar x\) is the mean of the masses and \(s^2\) their empirical variance with divisor \(n\). Report the convergence code, the numbers of evaluations of fn and gr, and the shape at the start and at the end of the search. What do the counts say about the search?
  2. Diagnose the fit with the gradient at the solution and a refit from the solution. How much lower is the NLL at the end of the refit? Use the stopping rule of BFGS in the theory to explain why the search stopped: compare the decrease of the NLL in its single step with the smallest decrease that counts as progress. Why did the step lower the NLL so little? (Look at the gradient at the starting values and at the correlation of the estimates of \(\log(k)\) and \(\log(r)\).)
  3. Try three remedies, each from the method-of-moments estimates: the masses in kilograms; the parameterisation with the mean \(\mu=k/r\) and the shape, with \(\log(\mu)\) and \(\log(k)\) as the parameters; and nlminb() with the masses in grams. Which of them reach the maximum? Compare the correlation of the estimates in the two parameterisations.
  4. Which of the remedies change the problem that the optimiser solves, and which only search again? Why do the estimates of the shape and of the mean not depend on the units, while the minimum of the NLL does?

1.1 Solution

1.1.1 Question 1

We select the Adelie penguins, remove the missing mass and write the NLL of the Gamma model as in Chapter 4, with the data in the element x:

# Load the penguin data (in R 4.5.0 and later) and keep the Adelie penguins.
data(penguins, package = "datasets")
adelie = penguins[penguins$species == "Adelie", ]

# One body mass is missing; remove it explicitly and keep the masses in grams.
sum(is.na(adelie$body_mass))
[1] 1
mass_g = adelie$body_mass[!is.na(adelie$body_mass)]
length(mass_g)
[1] 151
# Negative log-likelihood of the Gamma model with shape k and rate r.
gamma_nll = function(parms, data) {
  getAll(parms, data)
  shape = exp(log_shape)
  rate = exp(log_rate)
  -sum(dgamma(x = x, shape = shape, rate = rate, log = TRUE))
}

The method-of-moments estimates come from \(E[X]=k/r\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(X)=k/r^2\), which give \(k=\bar x^2/s^2\) and \(r=\bar x/s^2\). We start the fit there and look at the counts of evaluations and at the shape before and after the search:

# Method-of-moments estimates: the mean, the variance with divisor n, and the shape and
# rate (1/g) on the log scale.
mass_mean = mean(mass_g)
mass_variance = mean((mass_g - mass_mean)^2)
moments_start_g = list(
  log_shape = log(mass_mean^2 / mass_variance),
  log_rate = log(mass_mean / mass_variance)
)

# Process the NLL of the masses in grams and fit it from these values.
penguin_obj_g = MakeADFun(cmb(gamma_nll, list(x = mass_g)), moments_start_g, silent = TRUE)
penguin_fit_g = optim(
  par = penguin_obj_g$par,
  fn = penguin_obj_g$fn,
  gr = penguin_obj_g$gr,
  method = "BFGS"
)

# Convergence code, evaluations of fn and gr, and the shape at the start and at the end.
c(
  convergence = penguin_fit_g$convergence,
  penguin_fit_g$counts,
  shape_start = exp(penguin_obj_g$par[["log_shape"]]),
  shape_end = exp(penguin_fit_g$par[["log_shape"]])
)
convergence    function    gradient shape_start   shape_end 
    0.00000     8.00000     1.00000    65.56026    65.56283

The fit reports code 0, with 8 evaluations of fn and a single one of gr. BFGS evaluates the gradient once at the start and once after every step that counts as progress, so a single evaluation of gr means that no step counted as progress: the 8 evaluations of fn are the starting values and seven trial points of one line search. The shape changed only from 65.560 to 65.563. So the search took one tiny step and stopped.

The gradient, \(-0.22\) and \(-0.39\), is far from zero, so the search stopped on a slope. The refit moves on to a shape of 66.09 and its NLL is 0.0025 lower. The difference is small, but it shows that the first fit stopped before the minimum.

The stopping rule of BFGS in optim() counts a step as no progress when it lowers the NLL by less than \(\texttt{reltol}\times\left(|{-\ell(\boldsymbol\theta)}|+\texttt{reltol}\right)\), with the NLL at the start of the step and \(\texttt{reltol}=\sqrt{\epsilon}\) by default. We compare the decrease of the NLL in the single step with this threshold:

# Decrease of the NLL in the single step, and the smallest decrease that counts as
# progress, with the default reltol of optim().
reltol = sqrt(.Machine$double.eps)
nll_start_g = penguin_obj_g$fn(penguin_obj_g$par)
c(
  decrease = nll_start_g - penguin_fit_g$value,
  threshold = reltol * (abs(nll_start_g) + reltol)
)
    decrease    threshold 
1.641945e-05 1.695345e-05

The step lowered the NLL by \(1.64\times10^{-5}\), just below the threshold of \(1.70\times10^{-5}\), so it counted as no progress. After a step without progress BFGS discards its approximation of the curvature and tries a step along the negative gradient. This was the first step, which already went along the negative gradient, so the search stopped at once and returned code 0. The threshold is proportional to the NLL, which is about 1138 with the masses in grams.

Why did the step lower the NLL so little? The gradient at the starting values, and the correlation of the estimates at the maximum, show it:

[1,] -0.6138763 3.208625e-12

          log_shape  log_rate
log_shape 1.0000000 0.9962198
log_rate  0.9962198 1.0000000

At the method-of-moments estimates the mean of the model, \(k/r\), already equals the mean of the sample, so the slope with respect to the log rate is zero, and the first step goes along the log shape alone. The estimates of \(\log(k)\) and \(\log(r)\) have a correlation of 0.996: the NLL is a narrow valley along which the shape and the rate change together, keeping the mean close to the sample mean. A step in the shape alone changes the mean and goes across the valley, so the NLL rises steeply even for a short step, and the line search had to shorten the step to a change of the shape of about 0.003 before the NLL fell. This is the narrow, tilted valley of the theory, together with a tolerance that is large because the NLL is large.

1.1.3 Question 3

For the masses in kilograms the shape has no units and keeps its starting value, and the rate in 1/kg is 1000 times the rate in 1/g, so we add \(\log(1000)\) to the starting value of the log rate. We also calculate the threshold of the stopping rule in kilograms:

# The masses in kilograms, and the same method-of-moments estimates in these units.

With the mean as a parameter, the rate is \(k/\mu\). We call the NLL gamma_mean_nll() and start at the mean of the sample and the method-of-moments estimate of the shape, which are the same starting values as before, written with other parameters:

# Negative log-likelihood of the Gamma model with the mean mu (g) and the shape k.
gamma_mean_nll = function(parms, data) {
  getAll(parms, data)
  mu = exp(log_mu)
  shape = exp(log_shape)
  # The rate that gives the mean mu: E[X] = shape / rate.
  -sum(dgamma(x = x, shape = shape, rate = shape / mu, log = TRUE))
}
penguin_mean_obj = MakeADFun(
  cmb(gamma_mean_nll, list(x = mass_g)),
  list(log_mu = log(mass_mean), log_shape = moments_start_g$log_shape),
  silent = TRUE
)
penguin_mean_fit = optim(
  par = penguin_mean_obj$par,
  fn = penguin_mean_obj$fn,
  gr = penguin_mean_obj$gr,
  method = "BFGS"
)

nlminb() takes the object of question 1, with the masses in grams:

  start = penguin_obj_g$par,

1.1.4 Question 4

The change of units and the change of parameterisation change the problem that the optimiser solves. In kilograms the surface has the same shape, because the NLL differs from that in grams only by the constant \(151\times\log(1000)\), as Chapter 4 showed, but the NLL is smaller and so is the threshold of the relative tolerance. With the mean and the shape the surface has another shape, and the narrow tilted valley becomes one that is not tilted. The restart of question 2 and nlminb() solve the same problem again: the restart continues from where the first search stopped, and nlminb() follows another path on the same surface. In this example all of them work, but a change of units or of parameterisation helps every later fit of the model, whatever its starting values, and a new search only repairs this fit.

The estimates of the shape and of the mean do not depend on the units because they describe the same fitted distribution. The shape has no units, and the mean is a mass, 3,701 g or 3.70 kg; by invariance, the estimate of a function of the parameters is that function of their estimates. The minimum of the NLL depends on the units because a density is a probability per unit of the measurement: each mass in kilograms has a density 1000 times larger than in grams, so the log-likelihood rises by \(\log(1000)\) for each penguin.

2 Exercise 3: A variance among sites at zero

In Exercise 5 of the Chapter 7 practical we simulated 500 hypothetical samples of the log biomass of the herb layer (biomass in g/m²) in five quadrats at each of six sites, from a Normal model with a random intercept for each site,

\[ u_j\sim\operatorname{Normal}\left(0,\sigma^2_{\mathrm{site}}\right), \qquad Y_{ij}\mid u_j\sim\operatorname{Normal}\left(\mu+u_j,\,\sigma^2_{\mathrm{obs}}\right), \]

where \(Y_{ij}\) is the log biomass in quadrat \(i\) of site \(j\), with \(\mu=4\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{site}}=\sigma_{\mathrm{obs}}=1\), and estimated the variances by REML. In some samples the REML estimate of \(\sigma^2_{\mathrm{site}}\) was practically zero. In some of these the maximum of the likelihood lies at zero, and in others the fit stopped on its way towards zero although the maximum lies at a positive variance. Here we tell the two cases apart.

The code below repeats the simulation with the seed of Chapter 7, so that the samples are the same, but fits only REML, and it keeps every sample and the result of its fit. It needs the helper cmb(). As in Chapter 7, every fit starts from the values used to simulate:

# Joint NLL of a Normal model with a random intercept for each site (Chapter 7).
site_nll = function(parms, data) {
  getAll(parms, data)
  log_biomass = OBS(log_biomass)
  # Minus the log density of the deviations of the sites, before they are used.
  nll = -sum(dnorm(x = u, mean = 0, sd = exp(log_sd_site), log = TRUE))
  nll - sum(dnorm(x = log_biomass, mean = mu + u[site], sd = exp(log_sd_obs), log = TRUE))
}

# The design, five quadrats at each of six sites, and the values used to simulate, which
# are also the starting values of every fit. The log biomasses are placeholders.
n_sites = 6
site_index = rep(seq_len(n_sites), each = 5)
site_start = list(mu = 4, log_sd_site = log(1), log_sd_obs = log(1), u = rep(0, n_sites))
placeholder_obj = MakeADFun(
  cmb(site_nll, list(log_biomass = rep(0, times = 5 * n_sites), site = site_index)),
  site_start,
  random = "u",
  silent = TRUE
)

# 500 hypothetical samples (one column each) and the REML fit of each, with the mean
# integrated out together with the site deviations.
n_samples = 500
reml_samples = matrix(NA, nrow = 5 * n_sites, ncol = n_samples)
reml_results = data.frame(
  nll = rep(NA, n_samples),
  convergence = NA,
  log_sd_site = NA,
  log_sd_obs = NA
)
set.seed(751)
for (i in seq_len(n_samples)) {
  reml_samples[, i] = placeholder_obj$simulate(placeholder_obj$par)$log_biomass
  reml_obj = MakeADFun(
    cmb(site_nll, list(log_biomass = reml_samples[, i], site = site_index)),
    site_start,
    random = c("u", "mu"),
    silent = TRUE
  )
  reml_fit = optim(par = reml_obj$par, fn = reml_obj$fn, gr = reml_obj$gr, method = "BFGS")
  reml_results[i, ] = c(reml_fit$value, reml_fit$convergence, reml_fit$par)
}

# REML estimates of the variance among sites.
reml_results$var_site = exp(2 * reml_results$log_sd_site)

  3. Explain why the log scale puts a variance of zero at \(-\infty\), and why BFGS stops on its way there. When does the maximum of the likelihood lie at zero? A small function processes the NLL of sample i as in the simulation, and a loop calculates the two checks at the estimates of each of these samples, passing the estimates to sdreport() with par.fixed:

# The REML object of sample i, processed again with its data.
reml_object = function(i) {

2.1.2 Question 2

For each sample we fit the model from 10 random starting values and keep the fit with the lowest NLL, and we fit it with nlminb() from the values used to simulate, which are the starting values obj$par of the object. The seed is set once, before the loop over the samples:

# Refits of each sample from 10 random starting values and with nlminb().
refits = data.frame(
  sample = zero_samples,
  nll_bfgs = reml_results$nll[zero_samples],
  nll_starts = NA, var_starts = NA,
  nll_nlminb = NA, var_nlminb = NA
)
set.seed(831)
for (j in seq_along(zero_samples)) {
  obj = reml_object(zero_samples[j])
  # 10 fits from random starting values; keep the one with the lowest NLL.
  best_nll = Inf
  for (r in 1:10) {
    start = rnorm(2, mean = 0, sd = 1)
    names(start) = names(obj$par)
    fit = optim(par = start, fn = obj$fn, gr = obj$gr, method = "BFGS")
    if (fit$value < best_nll) {
      best_nll = fit$value
      best_par = fit$par
    }
  }
  refits$nll_starts[j] = best_nll
  refits$var_starts[j] = exp(2 * best_par[["log_sd_site"]])
  # nlminb() from the values used to simulate.
  fit = nlminb(start = obj$par, objective = obj$fn, gradient = obj$gr)
  refits$nll_nlminb[j] = fit$objective
  refits$var_nlminb[j] = exp(2 * fit$par[["log_sd_site"]])
}

2.1.3 Question 3

The standard deviation among sites is optimised as \(\log(\sigma_{\mathrm{site}})\), and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{site}}=e^{\log(\sigma_{\mathrm{site}})}\) is positive for every finite value of its logarithm. A variance of zero is reached only in the limit \(\log(\sigma_{\mathrm{site}})\to-\infty\), so it is a boundary that lies at \(-\infty\) on the optimised scale (theory). As \(\log(\sigma_{\mathrm{site}})\) falls, the variance shrinks towards zero and the NLL approaches the value of the model without site effects ever more slowly. Far enough out, a step lowers or changes the NLL by less than the relative tolerance, and BFGS stops with code 0, at a variance that is practically zero and depends on where the search happened to stop.

The same plateau causes both kinds of fits. When the maximum lies at zero, the NLL falls all the way towards the plateau: the fit stopped on its way to the maximum, the NLL curves upwards there, and the Hessian is positive definite, with a huge standard error of \(\log(\sigma_{\mathrm{site}})\). When the maximum lies at a positive variance, the NLL has a minimum and rises from it towards the plateau, so a search that a long step took past the minimum, far out on the plateau, finds no slope to lead it back. There the NLL curves downwards as it levels off, and the Hessian is not positive definite.

The maximum lies at zero when the sites differ less than the quadrats alone would make them differ. Each site mean is the mean of five quadrats, so even without site effects the site means vary with a variance of \(\sigma^2_{\mathrm{obs}}/5\); when the six observed means vary less than that, no positive variance among sites makes the data more probable. The closed form of this balanced design makes the comparison exact:

# Closed form of the REML estimate for this balanced Normal design (a special case): the
# variance of the six site means minus the variance within sites divided by five.
closed_form = rep(NA, length(zero_samples))
for (j in seq_along(zero_samples)) {
  y = reml_samples[, zero_samples[j]]
  site_means = tapply(y, site_index, mean)
  var_means = var(site_means)
  var_within = sum((y - site_means[site_index])^2) / (5 * n_sites - n_sites)
  closed_form[j] = max(0, var_means - var_within / 5)
}

We replace the estimates of the samples that moved by those of nlminb(), and compare the mean of the 500 estimates before and after, with the Monte Carlo standard error of the mean:

# REML estimates of the variance among sites, with the refits of nlminb() for the samples

The mean rises from 0.954 to 0.955, a change of less than 0.001, far smaller than its Monte Carlo standard error of 0.034. The failed fits were in samples whose maximum lies close to zero anyway, so correcting them hardly changes the mean, as Chapter 7 expected, and its conclusion stands: in this design REML removes most or all of the bias of maximum likelihood. A simulation study should still count the fits that stop at a boundary and say how it treated them, because failures in samples with larger variances would bias its results.

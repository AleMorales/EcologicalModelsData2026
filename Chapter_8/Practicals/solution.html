Chapter 8: Numerical optimisation — solutions

Published

November 4, 2026

The fully worked first exercise is on the student practical page. These solutions use the same data, tasks and seeds as Exercises 2 and 3 on that page. All three exercises are the core of the practical, and each follows the four steps of a diagnosis: the symptom, the diagnosis, the remedy and the confirmation.

As on that page, we load RTMB first and RTMBdist second, and define the helper cmb() that connects a likelihood to a data set:

# Load RTMB first and RTMBdist second.
library(RTMB)
library(RTMBdist)

# Connect a likelihood to a data set.
cmb = function(f, d) function(p) f(p, d)

1 Exercise 2: Body mass of Adelie penguins

The data set penguins of the package datasets (R 4.5.0 and later) contains the body masses, in grams, of adult penguins of three species near Palmer Station, Antarctica. We fit a Gamma model with shape \(k\) and rate \(r\), the model of the tree volumes in Exercise 4 of the Chapter 4 practical, to the body masses of the 151 Adelie penguins whose mass was recorded,

\[ X_i \sim \operatorname{Gamma}(k, r), \qquad E[X_i]=\frac{k}{r}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X_i)=\frac{k}{r^2}, \]

where \(X_i\) is the body mass of penguin \(i\), with \(\log(k)\) and \(\log(r)\) as the parameters. Started at the method-of-moments estimates, the fit to the masses in grams stops after a single step and reports convergence code 0. Load the data with data(penguins, package = "datasets"), keep the Adelie penguins and remove the one penguin whose mass is missing (NA).

  1. Fit the Gamma model to the masses in grams with dgamma(), starting at the method-of-moments estimates \(k=\bar x^2/s^2\) and \(r=\bar x/s^2\), where \(\bar x\) is the mean of the masses and \(s^2\) their empirical variance with divisor \(n\). Report the convergence code, the numbers of evaluations of fn and gr, and the shape at the start and at the end of the search. What do the counts say about the search? Hint: the NLL is the one of Exercise 4 of the Chapter 4 practical. The element counts of the result of optim() is explained in Exact and approximate gradients; BFGS evaluates the gradient at its starting values and again after every step that counts as progress (Stopping rules and convergence codes).
  2. Diagnose the fit with the gradient at the solution and a refit from the solution. How much lower is the NLL at the end of the refit? Then use the stopping rule of BFGS to explain why the first search stopped: compare the decrease of the NLL in its single step with the smallest decrease that counts as progress. Hint: the checks are those of Diagnosing a fit. The smallest decrease that counts as progress is the formula in Stopping rules and convergence codes, where \(|{-\ell(\boldsymbol\theta)}|\) is the NLL at the start of the step, obj$fn(obj$par), and reltol is sqrt(.Machine$double.eps).
  3. Try three remedies, each from the method-of-moments estimates: (a) the masses in kilograms; (b) the parameterisation with the mean \(\mu=k/r\) and the shape, with \(\log(\mu)\) and \(\log(k)\) as the parameters, starting at the mean of the masses and the method-of-moments estimate of the shape; and (c) nlminb() with the masses in grams. Which of them reach the maximum that the refit of question 2 found? Hint: for (a), calculate the method-of-moments estimates again from the masses in kilograms. For (b), the rate is \(k/\mu\); Exercise 1 of the Chapter 6 practical wrote the Gamma model with its mean in the same way, through scale = mu / shape (Parameterisation). For (c), see Other local optimisers. The minimum of the NLL in kilograms differs from that in grams, so compare the estimates of the shape, which has no units.
  4. Explain why each remedy reaches the maximum. For the masses in kilograms, calculate the smallest decrease that counts as progress at the starting values and compare it with that in grams and with the decrease of the single step of question 2. Why is the NLL smaller in kilograms? For the parameterisation, compare the correlation of the estimates of \(\log(k)\) and \(\log(r)\) with that of the estimates of \(\log(\mu)\) and \(\log(k)\), both at the maximum. Which of the three remedies and the refit of question 2 change the problem that the optimiser solves, and which search the same problem again? Hint: the effect of the units on the log-likelihood is explained in The likelihood function of Chapter 4. The correlations are cov2cor() of the element cov.fixed of sdreport(), as in Exercise 4 of the Chapter 4 practical, with par.fixed set to the estimates at the maximum. See Scaling and parameterisation.

1.1 Solution

1.1.1 Question 1

We select the Adelie penguins, remove the missing mass and write the NLL of the Gamma model as in Chapter 4, with the data in the element x:

# Load the penguin data (in R 4.5.0 and later) and keep the Adelie penguins.
data(penguins, package = "datasets")
adelie = penguins[penguins$species == "Adelie", ]

# One body mass is missing; remove it and keep the masses in grams.
mass_g = adelie$body_mass[!is.na(adelie$body_mass)]
length(mass_g)
[1] 151
# Negative log-likelihood of the Gamma model with shape k and rate r.
gamma_nll = function(parms, data) {
  getAll(parms, data)
  shape = exp(log_shape)
  rate = exp(log_rate)
  -sum(dgamma(x = x, shape = shape, rate = rate, log = TRUE))
}

The method-of-moments estimates come from \(E[X]=k/r\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(X)=k/r^2\), which give \(k=\bar x^2/s^2\) and \(r=\bar x/s^2\). Question 3 needs them again for the masses in kilograms, so we calculate them with a small function that returns them as starting values on the log scale:

# Method-of-moments estimates of the shape and the rate, as starting values on the log
# scale; the variance has divisor n.
gamma_mom_start = function(x) {
  mu_mom = mean(x)
  var_mom = mean((x - mu_mom)^2)
  list(log_shape = log(mu_mom^2 / var_mom), log_rate = log(mu_mom / var_mom))
}

We fit the model from these values and look at the counts of evaluations and at the shape before and after the search:

# Process the NLL of the masses in grams with the method-of-moments estimates, and fit it.
penguin_obj_g = MakeADFun(
  cmb(gamma_nll, list(x = mass_g)),
  gamma_mom_start(mass_g),
  silent = TRUE
)
penguin_fit_g = optim(
  par = penguin_obj_g$par,
  fn = penguin_obj_g$fn,
  gr = penguin_obj_g$gr,
  method = "BFGS"
)

# Convergence code, evaluations of fn and gr, and the shape at the start and at the end.
c(
  convergence = penguin_fit_g$convergence,
  penguin_fit_g$counts,
  shape_start = exp(penguin_obj_g$par[["log_shape"]]),
  shape_end = exp(penguin_fit_g$par[["log_shape"]])
)
convergence    function    gradient shape_start   shape_end 
    0.00000     8.00000     1.00000    65.56026    65.56283

The fit reports code 0, with 8 evaluations of fn and a single one of gr. BFGS evaluates the gradient at its starting values and again after every step that counts as progress, so a single evaluation of gr means that no step counted as progress: the 8 evaluations of fn are the starting values and the trial points of one line search. The shape changed only from 65.560 to 65.563. So the search took one tiny step and stopped.

The gradient, \(-0.22\) and \(-0.39\), is far from zero, so the search stopped on a slope. The refit moves on to a shape of 66.09, and its NLL is 0.0025 lower. The difference is small, but it shows that the first fit stopped before the minimum.

The stopping rule of BFGS in optim() counts a step as no progress when it lowers the NLL by less than \(\texttt{reltol}\times\left(|{-\ell(\boldsymbol\theta)}|+\texttt{reltol}\right)\), with the NLL at the start of the step and \(\texttt{reltol}=\sqrt{\epsilon}\) by default. We compare the decrease of the NLL in the single step with this threshold:

# Decrease of the NLL in the single step, and the smallest decrease that counts as
# progress, with the default reltol of optim().
reltol = sqrt(.Machine$double.eps)
nll_start_g = penguin_obj_g$fn(penguin_obj_g$par)
c(
  decrease = nll_start_g - penguin_fit_g$value,
  threshold = reltol * (abs(nll_start_g) + reltol)
)
    decrease    threshold 
1.641945e-05 1.695345e-05

The step lowered the NLL by \(1.64\times10^{-5}\), just below the threshold of \(1.70\times10^{-5}\), so it counted as no progress. After a step without progress BFGS discards its approximation of the curvature and tries a step along the negative gradient. This was the first step, which already went along the negative gradient, so the search stopped at once and returned code 0. The threshold is proportional to the NLL, which is about 1138 with the masses in grams.

1.1.3 Question 3

For remedy (a) we divide the masses by 1000 and calculate the method-of-moments estimates again from them:

# (a) The masses in kilograms, from the method-of-moments estimates in these units.
mass_kg = mass_g / 1000
penguin_obj_kg = MakeADFun(
  cmb(gamma_nll, list(x = mass_kg)),
  gamma_mom_start(mass_kg),
  silent = TRUE
)
penguin_fit_kg = optim(
  par = penguin_obj_kg$par,
  fn = penguin_obj_kg$fn,
  gr = penguin_obj_kg$gr,
  method = "BFGS"
)

For remedy (b) the NLL takes the mean and the shape, and calculates the rate from them as \(k/\mu\). We call it gamma_mean_nll() and start at the mean of the masses and the method-of-moments estimate of the shape, which are the same starting values as before, written with other parameters:

# (b) Negative log-likelihood of the Gamma model with the mean mu (g) and the shape k.
gamma_mean_nll = function(parms, data) {
  getAll(parms, data)
  mu = exp(log_mu)
  shape = exp(log_shape)
  # The rate that gives the mean mu: E[X] = shape / rate.
  -sum(dgamma(x = x, shape = shape, rate = shape / mu, log = TRUE))
}
penguin_mean_obj = MakeADFun(
  cmb(gamma_mean_nll, list(x = mass_g)),
  list(log_mu = log(mean(mass_g)), log_shape = gamma_mom_start(mass_g)$log_shape),
  silent = TRUE
)
penguin_mean_fit = optim(
  par = penguin_mean_obj$par,
  fn = penguin_mean_obj$fn,
  gr = penguin_mean_obj$gr,
  method = "BFGS"
)

Remedy (c), nlminb(), takes the object of question 1, with the masses in grams:

# (c) nlminb() from the method-of-moments estimates, with the masses in grams.
penguin_nlminb_g = nlminb(
  start = penguin_obj_g$par,
  objective = penguin_obj_g$fn,
  gradient = penguin_obj_g$gr

1.1.4 Question 4

For the masses in kilograms we calculate the threshold of the stopping rule at the starting values, as in question 2:

# Smallest decrease that counts as progress at the starting values, in kilograms.
nll_start_kg = penguin_obj_kg$fn(penguin_obj_kg$par)
c(nll_start = nll_start_kg, threshold = reltol * (abs(nll_start_kg) + reltol))
   nll_start    threshold 
9.465560e+01 1.410478e-06

In kilograms the NLL at the starting values is about 95, against 1138 in grams. A density is a probability per unit of the measurement, so the density of each mass in kilograms is 1000 times larger than in grams, and the log-likelihood rises by \(\log(1000)\) for each penguin, \(151\times\log(1000)\approx1043\) in all. The threshold falls with the NLL, to \(1.4\times10^{-6}\). The surface in kilograms is that in grams moved by \(\log(1000)\) along the log rate and lowered by this constant, so the gradient and the first step are the same as in grams, and so is its decrease of \(1.64\times10^{-5}\). In kilograms this decrease counts as progress, and BFGS goes on to the maximum.

For the parameterisation we compare the correlations of the estimates at the maximum, with the refit of question 2 for the shape and the rate:

# Correlation of the estimates of log(k) and log(r) at the maximum.
cov2cor(sdreport(penguin_obj_g, par.fixed = penguin_restart_g$par)$cov.fixed)
          log_shape  log_rate
log_shape 1.0000000 0.9962198
log_rate  0.9962198 1.0000000
# Correlation of the estimates of log(mu) and log(k) at the maximum.
cov2cor(sdreport(penguin_mean_obj, par.fixed = penguin_mean_fit$par)$cov.fixed)
                 log_mu     log_shape
log_mu     1.000000e+00 -1.281074e-06
log_shape -1.281074e-06  1.000000e+00

With the shape and the rate the correlation is 0.996. The NLL is a narrow valley, tilted with respect to the axes, along which the shape and the rate change together and keep the mean \(k/r\) close to the mean of the masses. The steepest direction points across this valley, so a step along it soon goes uphill, and the line search had to shorten the first step to a change of the shape of about 0.003 before the NLL fell at all. With the mean and the shape the correlation is practically zero: the valley is not tilted, and the steps of BFGS can follow it.

The change of units and the change of parameterisation change the problem that the optimiser solves. In kilograms the surface has the same shape, but the NLL is smaller and so is the threshold of the stopping rule. With the mean and the shape the surface has another shape, a valley that is not tilted. The refit of question 2 and nlminb() search the same problem again: the refit continues from where the first search stopped, and nlminb() controls its steps differently and follows another path on the same surface. Here all of them work, but a change of units or of parameterisation changes the surface for every later fit of the model, whereas a new search repairs only this fit.

2 Exercise 3: A variance among sites at zero

In Exercise 5 of the Chapter 7 practical we simulated 500 hypothetical samples of the log biomass of the herb layer (biomass in g/m²) in five quadrats at each of six sites, from a Normal model with a random intercept for each site,

\[ u_j\sim\operatorname{Normal}\left(0,\sigma^2_{\mathrm{site}}\right), \qquad Y_{ij}\mid u_j\sim\operatorname{Normal}\left(\mu+u_j,\,\sigma^2_{\mathrm{obs}}\right), \]

where \(Y_{ij}\) is the log biomass in quadrat \(i\) of site \(j\), with \(\mu=4\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{site}}=\sigma_{\mathrm{obs}}=1\), and estimated the variances by REML. In some samples the REML estimate of \(\sigma^2_{\mathrm{site}}\) was practically zero. In some of these the maximum of the likelihood lies at zero, and in others the fit stopped on its way towards zero although the maximum lies at a positive variance. Here we tell the two cases apart. That exercise was extra practice; if you skipped it, the code below gives you the samples, and Estimates at a boundary explains the two cases.

The code below repeats the simulation with the seed of Chapter 7, so that the samples are the same, but fits only REML, and it keeps every sample and the result of its fit. It needs the helper cmb(). As in Chapter 7, every fit starts from the values used to simulate:

# Joint NLL of a Normal model with a random intercept for each site (Chapter 7).
site_nll = function(parms, data) {
  getAll(parms, data)
  log_biomass = OBS(log_biomass)
  # Minus the log density of the deviations of the sites, before they are used.
  nll = -sum(dnorm(x = u, mean = 0, sd = exp(log_sd_site), log = TRUE))
  nll - sum(dnorm(x = log_biomass, mean = mu + u[site], sd = exp(log_sd_obs), log = TRUE))
}

# The design, five quadrats at each of six sites, and the values used to simulate, which
# are also the starting values of every fit. The log biomasses are placeholders.
n_sites = 6
site_index = rep(seq_len(n_sites), each = 5)
site_start = list(mu = 4, log_sd_site = log(1), log_sd_obs = log(1), u = rep(0, n_sites))
placeholder_obj = MakeADFun(
  cmb(site_nll, list(log_biomass = rep(0, times = 5 * n_sites), site = site_index)),
  site_start,
  random = "u",
  silent = TRUE
)

# 500 hypothetical samples (one column each) and the REML fit of each, with the mean
# integrated out together with the site deviations.
n_samples = 500
reml_samples = matrix(NA, nrow = 5 * n_sites, ncol = n_samples)
reml_results = data.frame(
  nll = rep(NA, n_samples),
  convergence = NA,
  log_sd_site = NA,
  log_sd_obs = NA
)
set.seed(751)
for (i in seq_len(n_samples)) {
  reml_samples[, i] = placeholder_obj$simulate(placeholder_obj$par)$log_biomass
  reml_obj = MakeADFun(
    cmb(site_nll, list(log_biomass = reml_samples[, i], site = site_index)),
    site_start,
    random = c("u", "mu"),
    silent = TRUE
  )
  reml_fit = optim(par = reml_obj$par, fn = reml_obj$fn, gr = reml_obj$gr, method = "BFGS")
  reml_results[i, ] = c(reml_fit$value, reml_fit$convergence, reml_fit$par)
}

# REML estimates of the variance among sites.
reml_results$var_site = exp(2 * reml_results$log_sd_site)
  1. Count the samples in which the REML estimate of \(\sigma^2_{\mathrm{site}}\) is below 0.001, which we call practically zero, and check their convergence codes. For each of these samples, process the NLL again with its data, and calculate the largest absolute element of the gradient at the estimates and the element pdHess of sdreport(). What do the two checks show? Hint: which() gives the numbers of these samples. Process the NLL as in the loop above, with random = c("u", "mu"). The checks are those of Diagnosing a fit; the estimates of a sample are two columns of reml_results, which unlist() turns into a vector, as in question 3 of Exercise 1, and sdreport() needs them as par.fixed.
  2. Refit each of these samples from 10 sets of random starting values, with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{site}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{obs}}\) (standard deviations of the log biomass) between 0.1 and 10, drawn uniformly on the log scale (set seed 831 once, before the loop over the samples), and keep the refit with the lowest NLL. In how many samples does this refit end at a variance above 0.001 with a lower NLL than the fit of the simulation? Compare these samples with the result of pdHess in question 1. Hint: see Starting values and multiple starts and question 3 of Exercise 1. Here the loop over the starting values sits inside the loop over the samples; store the NLL and the estimate of \(\log(\sigma_{\mathrm{site}})\) of each of the 10 fits, and which.min() finds the best.
  3. Explain why the log scale puts a variance of zero at \(-\infty\), and why BFGS stops on its way there. When does the maximum of the likelihood lie at zero? For a balanced Normal design such as this one, the REML estimate has a closed form, \(\max\{0,\,s^2_{\mathrm{m}}-s^2_{\mathrm{w}}/5\}\), where \(s^2_{\mathrm{m}}\) is the variance of the six site means (divisor 5) and \(s^2_{\mathrm{w}}\) the variance of the quadrats around the means of their sites (divisor 24). This is an exact result of this special design, not of REML in general. Calculate it for the samples of question 1 and compare it with the refits of question 2. Hint: see Estimates at a boundary. For the closed form, tapply() (question 5 of Exercise 1 of the Chapter 7 practical) gives the six site means, and site_means[site_index] repeats the mean of its site for each of the 30 quadrats.

In 28 of the 500 samples the REML estimate of \(\sigma^2_{\mathrm{site}}\) is below 0.001, and all of these fits report code 0. To check a fit we need its object again. Question 2 needs the objects as well, so a small function processes the NLL of sample i as in the simulation:

# The REML object of sample i, processed again with its data.
reml_object = function(i) {
  MakeADFun(
    cmb(site_nll, list(log_biomass = reml_samples[, i], site = site_index)),
    site_start,
    random = c("u", "mu"),
    silent = TRUE
  )
}

A loop calculates the two checks at the estimates of each of these samples, passing the estimates to sdreport() with par.fixed:

# Largest absolute gradient and the check of the Hessian at the estimates of each sample.
zero_checks = data.frame(
  sample = zero_samples,
  max_gradient = NA,
  pd_hess = NA
)
for (j in seq_along(zero_samples)) {
  obj = reml_object(zero_samples[j])
  estimates = unlist(reml_results[zero_samples[j], c("log_sd_site", "log_sd_obs")])
  zero_checks$max_gradient[j] = max(abs(obj$gr(estimates)))
  zero_checks$pd_hess[j] = sdreport(obj, par.fixed = estimates)$pdHess
}

2.1.2 Question 2

The box of starting values is the same for both standard deviations, from \(\log(0.1)\) to \(\log(10)\) on the optimised scale. For each sample we fit the model from 10 starting values drawn in this box, store the NLL and the estimate of \(\log(\sigma_{\mathrm{site}})\) of each fit, and keep the fit with the lowest NLL. The seed is set once, before the loop over the samples:

# Refits of each sample from 10 random starting values: the lowest NLL and its variance
# among sites.
refits = data.frame(
  sample = zero_samples,
  nll_simulation = reml_results$nll[zero_samples],
  nll_best = NA,
  var_best = NA
)
set.seed(831)
for (j in seq_along(zero_samples)) {
  obj = reml_object(zero_samples[j])
  start_nll = rep(NA, 10)
  start_log_sd_site = rep(NA, 10)
  for (r in 1:10) {
    # Both log standard deviations drawn between log(0.1) and log(10).
    start = runif(2, min = log(0.1), max = log(10))
    names(start) = names(obj$par)
    fit = optim(par = start, fn = obj$fn, gr = obj$gr, method = "BFGS")
    start_nll[r] = fit$value
    start_log_sd_site[r] = fit$par[["log_sd_site"]]
  }
  # Keep the fit with the lowest NLL.
  best = which.min(start_nll)
  refits$nll_best[j] = start_nll[best]
  refits$var_best[j] = exp(2 * start_log_sd_site[best])
}

2.1.3 Question 3

The standard deviation among sites is optimised as \(\log(\sigma_{\mathrm{site}})\), and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{site}}=e^{\log(\sigma_{\mathrm{site}})}\) is positive for every finite value of its logarithm. A variance of zero is reached only in the limit \(\log(\sigma_{\mathrm{site}})\to-\infty\), so it is a boundary that lies at \(-\infty\) on the optimised scale. As \(\log(\sigma_{\mathrm{site}})\) falls, the variance shrinks towards zero and the NLL approaches the value of the model without site effects ever more slowly. Far enough out, a step changes the NLL by less than the relative tolerance, and BFGS stops with code 0, at a variance that is practically zero and depends on where the search happened to stop.

The same plateau explains the two kinds of fits of question 1. When the maximum lies at zero, the NLL falls all the way towards the plateau, so it curves upwards where the fit stopped, and the Hessian is positive definite although the fit is still on its way to the maximum. When the maximum lies at a positive variance, the NLL rises from that minimum towards the plateau. A search that a long step took past the minimum finds no slope on the plateau to lead it back, and there the NLL curves downwards as it levels off, so the Hessian is not positive definite.

The maximum lies at zero when the sites differ less than the quadrats alone would make them differ. Each site mean is the mean of five quadrats, so even without site effects the site means vary with a variance of \(\sigma^2_{\mathrm{obs}}/5\); when the six observed means vary less than that, no positive variance among sites makes the data more probable. The closed form of this balanced design makes the comparison exact:

# Closed form of the REML estimate for this balanced Normal design (a special case): the
# variance of the six site means minus the variance within sites divided by five.
closed_form = rep(NA, length(zero_samples))
for (j in seq_along(zero_samples)) {
  y = reml_samples[, zero_samples[j]]
  site_means = tapply(y, site_index, mean)
  var_means = var(site_means)
  var_within = sum((y - site_means[site_index])^2) / (5 * n_sites - n_sites)
  closed_form[j] = max(0, var_means - var_within / 5)
}

The closed form is zero in the 19 samples whose Hessian was positive definite. In these the maximum lies at zero, the fits of the simulation were on their way to it, and the estimate is reported as zero. In the other 9 the closed form is positive: these fits were failures, and the refits found the maximum. Where the maximum lies above 0.01, the refits agree with the closed form to three digits; below 0.01 (samples 110, 156, 221 and 251) they differ by up to 15%, because so close to zero the NLL is very flat and the refits stop a little away from the maximum. The closed form exists only for this special design, but the check of the Hessian and the refits tell the two cases apart in the same way, and they can be used for any model.

The maxima of the failed fits all lie below 0.16, so replacing their estimates could raise the mean of the 500 REML estimates by at most \(9\times0.16/500\approx0.003\), far less than its Monte Carlo standard error of about 0.034 in Chapter 7. The conclusion of Chapter 7 about the bias of REML stands.

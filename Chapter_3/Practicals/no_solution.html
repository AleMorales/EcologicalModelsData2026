Chapter 3: From samples to estimates

Published

October 7, 2026

Exercise 1 uses field data on barn owl nests and the remaining exercises use simulated ecological measurements. Work through the first example before trying the remaining exercises. The ideas and notation are introduced in the Chapter 3 theory.

The distribution functions of the practical come from the packages RTMB and RTMBdist, which provide the distribution functions used in the whole course, including some that base R lacks. Chapter 4 explains what else they do. Load them once, RTMB first:

# Load RTMB first and RTMBdist second.
library(RTMB)
library(RTMBdist)

1 Exercise 1: Calls of barn owl nestlings

The data set Owls in the package glmmTMB contains observations of barn owl nests (Roulin and Bersier 2007). For each visit of a parent to a nest, the variable SiblingNegotiation is the number of calls made by the nestlings. We keep the visits of the female parent to nests where the nestlings had been fed (Satiated), so that all counts can reasonably share one mean:

# Load the Owls data from the glmmTMB package without attaching the package.
data(Owls, package = "glmmTMB")

# Keep the visits of the female parent to nests with satiated nestlings.
satiated_female = Owls[Owls$FoodTreatment == "Satiated" & Owls$SexParent == "Female", ]

# Number of calls at each of these visits.
call_counts = satiated_female$SiblingNegotiation

Let \(X_i\) be the number of calls at visit \(i\). The counts are much more variable than a Poisson distribution allows, so we use a Negative Binomial model with mean \(\mu\) and shape \(k\),

\[ X_i \sim \operatorname{NegBin}(\mu, k), \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X_i)=\mu+\frac{\mu^2}{k}, \]

and assume that the visits are i.i.d.

  1. Estimate the parameters of the Negative Binomial using the method of moments.
  2. Calculate the estimated standard error of the mean. Explain how it differs from the standard deviation of the individual counts.
  3. Construct an approximate 95% Wald confidence interval for \(\mu\) using qnorm(). Interpret the interval and its confidence level. Name one model assumption that matters for this interpretation.

1.1 Solution

1.1.1 Question 1

The observational unit is one visit of the female parent to a nest. The model has two parameters. The mean \(\mu=E[X_i]\) is the expected number of calls per visit, and the shape \(k\) controls the overdispersion: the smaller \(k\) is, the larger the variance is relative to the mean. The method of moments needs two equations, so we equate the mean and the variance of the model with those of the empirical distribution. The first gives \(\hat\mu=\overline X\). The second is \(\hat\sigma^2=\hat\mu+\hat\mu^2/\hat k\), which we solve for the shape:

\[ \hat k = \frac{\hat\mu^2}{\hat\sigma^2-\hat\mu}, \]

where \(\hat\sigma^2=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_i-\overline X)^2\) is the variance of the empirical distribution. The estimate of \(k\) exists only if the variance of the sample is larger than its mean as it cannot be negative.

# Number of visits and the mean of the sample.
n_visits = length(call_counts)
mu_hat = mean(call_counts)

# Variance of the empirical distribution (divisor n), as in the method of moments.
variance_hat = mean((call_counts - mu_hat)^2)

# Solve variance = mu + mu^2 / k for the shape k.
k_hat = mu_hat^2 / (variance_hat - mu_hat)

c(n_visits = n_visits, mu_hat = mu_hat, variance_hat = variance_hat, k_hat = k_hat)
    n_visits       mu_hat variance_hat        k_hat 
 122.0000000    4.7540984   42.3493684    0.6011781

The 122 counts sum to 580, so the estimate of the mean is \(580/122\approx 4.75\) calls per visit. The variance of the sample is approximately 42.3, almost nine times the mean, which gives \(\hat k\approx 0.60\).

1.1.2 Question 2

For i.i.d. observations, \(\operatorname{Var}(\overline X)=\operatorname{Var}(X_i)/n\). Under the Negative Binomial model \(\operatorname{Var}(X_i)=\mu+\mu^2/k\), and we do not know either parameter, so we insert the estimates:

\[ \widehat{\operatorname{SE}}(\hat\mu) = \sqrt{\frac{\hat\mu+\hat\mu^2/\hat k}{n}}. \]

# Plug the estimates of the mean and the shape into the standard error.
estimated_se = sqrt((mu_hat + mu_hat^2 / k_hat) / n_visits)

# Standard deviation of the individual counts.
count_sd = sd(call_counts)

# Standard error that a Poisson model would give, for comparison.
poisson_se = sqrt(mu_hat / n_visits)

c(estimated_se = estimated_se, count_sd = count_sd, poisson_se = poisson_se)
estimated_se     count_sd   poisson_se 
   0.5891740    6.5344750    0.1974032

The estimated standard error is approximately 0.59 calls per visit. Because \(\hat k\) comes from the method of moments, \(\hat\mu+\hat\mu^2/\hat k\) is the variance of the sample, so this value is the same as \(\sqrt{\hat\sigma^2/n}\). It describes the variation of the estimated mean count across hypothetical experiments with 122 visits each. The standard deviation of the counts, approximately 6.5 calls, describes the variation among the individual visits; it does not decrease when we observe more visits, whereas the standard error does. The Poisson formula would give 0.20 calls per visit, about a third of the value above, because it assumes that the variance equals the mean and ignores the overdispersion.

1.1.3 Question 3

For a Wald interval, use the 97.5th percentile of a standard Normal distribution on each side of the estimate:

# 0.975 quantile of the standard Normal distribution.
critical_value = qnorm(p = 0.975)

# Lower and upper endpoints of the interval for the mean number of calls.
mu_interval = c(
  lower = mu_hat - critical_value * estimated_se,
  upper = mu_hat + critical_value * estimated_se
)
mu_interval
   lower    upper 
3.599339 5.908858

The endpoints are approximately 3.60 and 5.91 calls per visit. This is a range for the mean count per visit, and most individual visits fall outside it. The 95% refers to the procedure: if we repeated the study many times and calculated an interval from each sample in this way, about 95% of the intervals would contain the fixed \(\mu\), provided that the model and the Normal approximation are adequate. The Normal approximation is less accurate when the counts are as skewed as these.

The assumption that matters most here is independence. The 122 visits come from only 24 nests, and visits to the same nest are likely to resemble each other. Treating the visits as i.i.d. is a simplification that we accept in this chapter; the models for observations nested in groups are the subject of Chapter 7.

2 Exercise 2: Asymptotic convergence under a Beta model

Let \(X_i\) be the fraction of the area of leaf \(i\) that has been eaten by herbivores, a continuous variable between 0 and 1. Assume that the leaves are i.i.d. and that \(X_i \sim \operatorname{Beta}(a, b)\) with \(a=2\) and \(b=6\) (shape1 and shape2 in R). You will simulate samples from this model with two sample sizes, 10 and 1,000 leaves, to see how the properties of a sample behave as the sample size grows (see Samples and distributions). The mean, variance and R functions of the Beta distribution are in the Beta entry of the supplement.

  1. Set seed 123 and simulate one sample of 10 leaves and one sample of 1,000 leaves. Calculate the mean, median and variance of each sample.
  2. Repeat question 1 four times, using the seeds 11, 22, 33 and 44. How much do the sample properties vary between repetitions?
  3. Calculate the mean, median and variance of the model. Compare the sample properties with these model values at the two sample sizes. Do the sample mean and the sample median approach the same value? What happens to the variation across repeated samples as the sample size increases?

3 Exercise 3: Variation in tree heights

The data set trees, which is part of the datasets package that comes with R, contains measurements of 31 felled black cherry trees. We use the column Height, the height of each tree in feet (ft). Let \(X_i\) be the height of tree \(i\) and assume that the trees are i.i.d. with \(X_i \sim \operatorname{Normal}(\mu,\sigma^2)\), where \(\mu\) is the mean of the model (in ft) and \(\sigma^2\) its variance (in ft²). Remember that the argument sd of rnorm() takes the standard deviation \(\sigma\). See Estimation of two parameters for the method of moments with this model.

With a single sample we cannot tell how far an estimate is from the parameter, because the parameter is unknown. To study the behaviour of an estimator we can simulate hypothetical experiments from a model whose parameters we know. Here we use the estimates from the observed trees as the parameters of that model (a plug-in estimate, as for the standard error in Standard errors). The simulation then shows how the estimators would behave if the fitted model had generated the data. The results approximate the behaviour of the estimators for the real trees, and the approximation is better the closer the estimates are to the parameters.

  1. Load the data with data(trees, package = "datasets"). Estimate \(\mu\) by method of moments. Estimate \(\sigma^2\) in two ways: as the variance of the empirical distribution (i.e. with \(n\) in the denominator) and with var() (i.e., with \(n-1\) in the denominator).
  2. Set seed 434 and simulate 2,000 hypothetical experiments, each with 31 trees from a Normal model whose mean and variance are the method-of-moments estimates of question 1. For each experiment, calculate both variance estimates. Compare their average values and simulated biases with the variance that you used to simulate.
  3. Calculate the mean squared error of each variance estimator in the simulation. Does knowing which estimator is unbiased, by itself, tell you which has the smaller mean squared error?

4 Exercise 4: Mean body mass and interval coverage

Suppose that the body masses (in grams) of small mammals are independent and follow a LogNormal model with meanlog = log(30) and sdlog = 0.8. As described in the LogNormal entry of the supplement, the model median on the original scale is \(e^{\mu}\) and the mean mass is \(e^{\mu+\sigma^2/2}\), where \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) describe log mass. In this exercise, you calculate approximate 95% Wald confidence intervals for the mean mass (see Confidence intervals) and check how often they contain it.

  1. Calculate the model mean and median mass in grams. Set seed 443, simulate one study of 12 animals, and calculate its sample mean, sample median, and estimated standard error of the sample mean (sd(mass) / sqrt(n)).
  2. For this study, calculate a 95% Wald interval for the original-scale mean mass using qnorm(). Interpret the interval. Why is its coverage only approximate for these LogNormal masses?
  3. Set seed 444. Simulate 2,000 studies of 12 animals and 2,000 studies of 100 animals. Calculate the Wald interval for every study and find the fraction of intervals that contain the model mean at each sample size. Compare the fractions with 0.95 and explain why coverage might change with sample size.
  4. State one reason why coverage in this simulation does not prove that the same procedure will work equally well for field data.

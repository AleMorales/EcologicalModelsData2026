Chapter 4: Maximum likelihood estimation

Published

October 6, 2026

Exercise 1 uses a laboratory experiment on the survival of tadpoles, Exercises 2 and 3 use field counts of glacier lily seedlings, and Exercise 4 uses measurements of trees and of penguins that come with R. Exercise 5 simulates hypothetical experiments from the models fitted in Exercise 4. Exercises 1 to 4 are the core of the practical and Exercise 5 is extra practice. The data of Exercises 1 to 3 are in the package emdbook, which you need to install once with install.packages("emdbook"). Work through the first example before trying the remaining exercises. The ideas and notation are introduced in the Chapter 4 theory.

1 Exercise 1: Survival of reed frog tadpoles

The data set ReedfrogPred in the package emdbook contains the results of laboratory experiments on the predation of tadpoles of the African reed frog Hyperolius spinigularis (Vonesh and Bolker 2005). Each row is a tank of 1.2 × 0.8 × 0.4 m. The variable density is the number of tadpoles that were placed in the tank at the start (10, 25 or 35) and surv is the number of tadpoles that survived. Questions 1 to 5 use the 24 tanks with predators, and question 6 also uses the 24 tanks without them:

# Load the reed frog data from the emdbook package without attaching the package.
data(ReedfrogPred, package = "emdbook")

# Keep the 24 tanks with predators.
predator_tanks = ReedfrogPred[ReedfrogPred$pred == "pred", ]

# Initial number of tadpoles in each tank and number of survivors, and the number of tanks.
tadpoles = predator_tanks$density
survivors = predator_tanks$surv
n_tanks = length(survivors)

Let \(X_i\) be the number of survivors in tank \(i\), which started with \(n_i\) tadpoles. We use a Binomial model with the same probability of survival \(p\) in all the tanks,

\[ X_i \sim \operatorname{Binomial}(n_i, p), \]

and assume that the tanks are independent. Note that the tanks are not identically distributed, because \(n_i\) differs among them. The likelihood is still the product of the contributions of the tanks, since the product only requires independence. Each contribution is calculated with the \(n_i\) of its own tank.

  1. On paper, write the likelihood contributions of the first three tanks (4, 9 and 7 survivors out of 10 tadpoles each) and their likelihood as a function of \(p\). Calculate the likelihood and the log-likelihood for \(p=0.5\) and for \(p=2/3\), and verify the results in R.
  2. On paper, derive the maximum likelihood estimator of \(p\) for all the tanks by differentiating the log-likelihood (the rules of differentiation are in the calculus refresher). Calculate the estimate.
  3. Write the negative log-likelihood (NLL) as a function of \(\operatorname{logit}p\) and minimise it with optim(). Check the convergence, report the estimate of \(p\) and the standard error on the logit scale, and calculate a 95% Wald interval for \(p\).
  4. Calculate the 95% likelihood interval for \(p\) with uniroot() and compare it with the Wald interval.
  5. Is the Binomial model adequate for these data? For each initial density, compare the variance of the number of survivors among tanks with the variance of the fitted model. What does the comparison imply for the intervals of questions 3 and 4?
  6. The experiment also had 24 tanks without predators. Fit a model with one probability of survival for all 48 tanks and a model with one probability for each treatment, and compare them with AIC and AICc. How much does the presence of predators change the probability of survival?

1.1 Solution

1.1.1 Question 1

The observational unit is one tank. The contribution of a tank to the likelihood is the probability that the Binomial model assigns to its number of survivors, \(P(X_i=x_i\mid p)=\binom{n_i}{x_i}p^{x_i}(1-p)^{n_i-x_i}\). The first three tanks started with 10 tadpoles each and had 4, 9 and 7 survivors, so their likelihood is

\[ \begin{aligned} L(p\mid 4,9,7) &= \binom{10}{4}p^{4}(1-p)^{6}\times \binom{10}{9}p^{9}(1-p)^{1}\times \binom{10}{7}p^{7}(1-p)^{3}\\ &= 210\times 10\times 120\;p^{20}(1-p)^{10} = 252{,}000\;p^{20}(1-p)^{10}. \end{aligned} \]

The exponents are the total number of survivors (20) and the total number of tadpoles that died (10) in the three tanks. For \(p=0.5\) the likelihood is \(252{,}000/2^{30}\approx 2.35\times10^{-4}\) and for \(p=2/3\) it is \(252{,}000\times 2^{20}/3^{30}\approx 1.28\times10^{-3}\). The log-likelihood is the logarithm of the same expression,

\[ \ell(p)=\log(252{,}000)+20\log p+10\log(1-p), \]

which gives \(12.437-20.794=-8.357\) for \(p=0.5\) and \(12.437-8.109-10.986=-6.658\) for \(p=2/3\). We can verify our math with R:

# The first three tanks: survivors and initial number of tadpoles.
first_survivors = survivors[1:3]
first_tadpoles = tadpoles[1:3]

# three tanks, which is the product of the contributions.

[1] 0.205078125 0.009765625 0.117187500
dbinom(x = first_survivors, size = first_tadpoles, prob = 2 / 3)
[1] 0.05690190 0.08670765 0.26012295
prod(dbinom(x = first_survivors, size = first_tadpoles, prob = 0.5))
[1] 0.0002346933

[1] 0.001283402
# Log-likelihood at the same two values: the sum of the log probabilities.

R returns the same values. The difference between the two log-likelihoods is 1.70, so the three counts are about 5.5 times more probable (\(e^{1.70}\)) under \(p=2/3\) than under \(p=0.5\). This is no coincidence, as 20 of the 30 tadpoles in these three tanks survived. The second tank, with 9 survivors out of 10, has a probability of only 0.0098 under \(p=0.5\).

1.1.2 Question 2

For all the tanks the log-likelihood is the sum of the logarithms of the 24 contributions:

\[ \begin{aligned} \ell(p) &= \sum_{i=1}^{24}\log\binom{n_i}{x_i} +\log(p)\sum_{i=1}^{24}x_i +\log(1-p)\sum_{i=1}^{24}(n_i-x_i),\\ \frac{d\ell}{dp} &= \frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{24}x_i-\frac{1}{1-p}\sum_{i=1}^{24}(n_i-x_i),\\ 0 &= \frac{1}{\hat p}\sum_{i=1}^{24}x_i-\frac{1}{1-\hat p}\sum_{i=1}^{24}(n_i-x_i) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \hat p=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{24}x_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{24}n_i}. \end{aligned} \]

The first term of the log-likelihood does not contain \(p\), so it disappears when we differentiate. To solve the last line, multiply both sides by \(\hat p(1-\hat p)\), which gives \((1-\hat p)\sum x_i=\hat p\sum(n_i-x_i)\), and collect the terms with \(\hat p\). The second derivative, \(-\sum x_i/p^2-\sum(n_i-x_i)/(1-p)^2\), is negative for every \(p\), so this is a maximum. In R:

# Total number of survivors and of tadpoles, and the maximum likelihood estimate from
# the derivation: p_hat = sum(x_i) / sum(n_i).
total_survivors = sum(survivors)
total_tadpoles = sum(tadpoles)
p_hat = total_survivors / total_tadpoles
c(survivors = total_survivors, tadpoles = total_tadpoles, p_hat = p_hat)
  survivors    tadpoles       p_hat 
264.0000000 560.0000000   0.4714286 
# The derivative of the log-likelihood, sum(x) / p - (sum(n) - sum(x)) / (1 - p),
# is zero at that value.
total_survivors / p_hat - (total_tadpoles - total_survivors) / (1 - p_hat)
[1] 0

In total 264 of the 560 tadpoles survived, so \(\hat p=264/560\approx 0.471\), and the derivative of the log-likelihood is zero at that value. The maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) is the fraction of all the tadpoles that survived. That is, the tanks are pooled, and a tank with 35 tadpoles weighs more in the estimate than a tank with 10.

1.1.3 Question 3

A probability lies between zero and one, so we let the optimiser search over \(\operatorname{logit}p\), which can take any real value, and convert it into a probability with plogis() inside the function. The NLL has two arguments for the data, and dbinom() receives the vector with the initial number of tadpoles of each tank in its argument size. We start the search at \(p=0.5\), whose logit is zero:

# Negative log-likelihood of the Binomial model with one survival probability p for all tanks.
# logit_p can take any real value and plogis() turns it into a probability.
# The tanks differ in the number of tadpoles n_i, so size is a vector.
binomial_nll = function(logit_p, survivors, tadpoles) {
  p = plogis(logit_p)
  -sum(dbinom(x = survivors, size = tadpoles, prob = p, log = TRUE))
}

# Fit starting from p = 0.5 (logit 0); hessian = TRUE is used for the standard error.
frog_fit = optim(
  par = 0,
  fn = binomial_nll,
  survivors = survivors,
  tadpoles = tadpoles,
  method = "BFGS",
  hessian = TRUE
)
frog_fit$convergence
[1] 0
# Estimate on the probability scale, and the minimum of the negative log-likelihood.
plogis(frog_fit$par)
[1] 0.4714286
frog_fit$value
[1] 98.93615

The convergence code is 0. By invariance, plogis() of the estimate of \(\operatorname{logit}p\) is the MLE of \(p\): 0.471, the value that we derived on paper. The minimum of the NLL is 98.94, so the maximum log-likelihood is \(-98.94\).

The Hessian that optim() returns refers to the parameter that it searched over, so its inverse is the approximate variance of the estimate of \(\operatorname{logit}p\). We calculate the Wald interval on the logit scale and transform its endpoints back with plogis():

# The Hessian is a 1 by 1 matrix; take its single element. For this model the second
# derivative of the NLL with respect to logit(p) is sum(n) * p * (1 - p).
frog_fit$hessian[1, 1]
[1] 139.5428
total_tadpoles * p_hat * (1 - p_hat)
[1] 139.5429
# Standard error on the logit scale and Wald interval on that scale, back-transformed
# to survival probabilities.
critical_value = qnorm(p = 0.975)
se_logit_p = sqrt(1 / frog_fit$hessian[1, 1])
wald_p = plogis(frog_fit$par + c(-1, 1) * critical_value * se_logit_p)
c(se_logit_p = se_logit_p)
se_logit_p 
0.08465376 
wald_p
[1] 0.4303732 0.5128741
# Standard error on the probability scale, from the formula for a proportion.
sqrt(p_hat * (1 - p_hat) / total_tadpoles)
[1] 0.02109433

The Hessian is 139.5, which agrees with the second derivative of the NLL calculated by hand. The standard error of the estimate of \(\operatorname{logit}p\) is \(1/\sqrt{139.5}\approx 0.085\) and the 95% Wald interval for the probability of survival goes from 0.430 to 0.513. As in the theory, we transformed the endpoints and not the standard error, because a standard error on the logit scale cannot be converted into a probability with plogis() (that requires the delta method of Chapter 6). For this model a standard error on the probability scale is available from a formula: the observed information for \(p\) is \(\sum n_i/[\hat p(1-\hat p)]\), so the standard error is \(\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/\sum n_i}\approx 0.021\).

1.1.4 Question 4

The model has one parameter, so the profile is the log-likelihood itself. The likelihood interval contains the values of \(p\) whose relative log-likelihood is above the cutoff of \(-1.92\) (see Profile likelihood intervals). We write the relative log-likelihood as a function of \(p\) and use uniroot() to find the two values at which it equals the cutoff. uniroot() needs an interval whose ends give values of opposite sign. The function that we give it is positive at the estimate, so we search between 0.2 and \(\hat p\) and between \(\hat p\) and 0.8, after checking that the relative log-likelihood is below the cutoff at 0.2 and at 0.8:

# Cutoff for a 95% interval: half the 95% quantile of a chi-squared distribution
# with one degree of freedom.
cutoff = qchisq(p = 0.95, df = 1) / 2

# Log-likelihood of the Binomial model as a function of p, relative to the maximum.
binomial_relative = function(p) {
  sum(dbinom(x = survivors, size = tadpoles, prob = p, log = TRUE)) +
    frog_fit$value
}

# Relative log-likelihood at the outer ends of the two search intervals.
binomial_relative(p = 0.2)
[1] -103.6945
binomial_relative(p = 0.8)
[1] -148.0559
# uniroot() finds where the relative log-likelihood equals -cutoff, below and above the
# estimate.
p_lower = uniroot(
  f = function(p) binomial_relative(p) + cutoff,
  interval = c(0.2, p_hat),
  tol = 1e-8
)$root
p_upper = uniroot(
  f = function(p) binomial_relative(p) + cutoff,
  interval = c(p_hat, 0.8),
  tol = 1e-8
)$root
c(lower = p_lower, upper = p_upper)
    lower     upper 
0.4302855 0.5128324

The relative log-likelihood is \(-103.7\) at \(p=0.2\) and \(-148.1\) at \(p=0.8\), far below the cutoff, so each search interval contains one crossing. The likelihood interval goes from 0.430 to 0.513, the same as the Wald interval to three decimals. The two methods agree because the sample is large: with 560 tadpoles the log-likelihood is very close to a parabola on the logit scale, which is the approximation behind the Wald interval. Under the Binomial model with independent tanks, we would report that the probability of survival is 0.47 with a 95% confidence interval from 0.43 to 0.51.

1.1.5 Question 5

The Binomial model gives the variance of the number of survivors, \(n_ip(1-p)\), as well as its mean, \(n_ip\). The experiment has several tanks for each initial density, so we can compare the variance of the number of survivors among the tanks of one density (calculated with var(), which uses the divisor \(n-1\)) with the variance of the fitted model:

# Variance of the number of survivors among tanks of the same initial density, and the
# variance that the Binomial model gives, n * p * (1 - p), with the estimated p.
densities = sort(unique(tadpoles))
variance_comparison = data.frame(
  density = densities,
  n_tanks = NA,
  observed_variance = NA,
  binomial_variance = NA
)
for (i in seq_along(densities)) {
  in_group = tadpoles == densities[i]
  variance_comparison$n_tanks[i] = sum(in_group)
  variance_comparison$observed_variance[i] = var(survivors[in_group])
  variance_comparison$binomial_variance[i] = densities[i] * p_hat * (1 - p_hat)
}
variance_comparison
  density n_tanks observed_variance binomial_variance
1      10       8          3.714286          2.491837
2      25       8         24.839286          6.229592
3      35       8         64.267857          8.721429

There are eight tanks at each density. With 10 tadpoles the variance among tanks is 3.7, against 2.5 under the model. With 25 tadpoles it is 24.8 against 6.2, and with 35 tadpoles 64.3 against 8.7. So the number of survivors varies more among tanks than the Binomial model allows, up to seven times more: the counts are overdispersed relative to the Binomial distribution (see Chapter 2). A Binomial model with one \(p\) assumes that every tadpole survives with the same probability and independently of the others. Tadpoles in the same tank share their predators and their conditions, so the probability of survival is likely to differ among tanks. The tanks also differed in the size of the tadpoles (the variable size), which is a candidate predictor of survival.

The consequence for questions 3 and 4 is that both intervals are too narrow. They are calculated as if the model had generated the data, that is, as if we had 560 independent tadpoles, while most of the variation is among the 24 tanks. The estimate of 0.47 is still the overall fraction of survivors. A model in which \(p\) varies among tanks is the Beta-Binomial distribution of the supplement, and models for observations that are grouped are the subject of Chapter 7.

1.1.6 Question 6

The two treatments belong to the same experiment, so we can compare them with a model for all 48 tanks. The first model is the one of question 3 applied to all the tanks, with one probability of survival (\(K=1\)). In the second model the tanks with predators have a probability \(p_{\mathrm{pred}}\) and the tanks without predators a probability \(p_{\mathrm{no}}\) (\(K=2\)), and the contribution of a tank is calculated with the probability of its treatment. We write the NLL of the second model with the logit of both probabilities as parameters. Both models are compared with AIC and with AICc, which now uses the 48 tanks as \(n\):

# All 48 tanks: survivors, initial number of tadpoles and treatment ("no" or "pred").
survivors_all = ReedfrogPred$surv
tadpoles_all = ReedfrogPred$density
treatment = ReedfrogPred$pred
n_tanks_all = length(survivors_all)
table(treatment)
treatment
  no pred 
  24   24 
# NLL of the model with one probability for each treatment. The first element of logit_p is
# the logit of the survival probability without predators and the second with predators.
treatment_nll = function(logit_p, survivors, tadpoles, treatment) {
  p = plogis(logit_p)
  prob = ifelse(treatment == "pred", p[2], p[1])
  -sum(dbinom(x = survivors, size = tadpoles, prob = prob, log = TRUE))
}

# Fit both models; binomial_nll() of question 3 serves for the model with one probability.
pooled_fit = optim(
  par = 0,
  fn = binomial_nll,
  survivors = survivors_all,
  tadpoles = tadpoles_all,
  method = "BFGS"
)
treatment_fit = optim(
  par = c(logit_p_no = 0, logit_p_pred = 0),
  fn = treatment_nll,
  survivors = survivors_all,
  tadpoles = tadpoles_all,
  treatment = treatment,
  method = "BFGS",
  hessian = TRUE
)

Both fits have convergence code 0. The model with one probability estimates 0.699, the fraction of the 1,120 tadpoles that survived. The model with two probabilities estimates 0.927 without predators and 0.471 with predators. These are the fractions of survivors in each group of tanks, as the derivation of question 2 predicts for a Binomial model, and the second is the estimate of question 3. The AIC is 574.5 for one probability and 274.3 for one probability per treatment, and the AICc is 574.6 and 274.5. The difference of about 300 units is far larger than the two units that the additional parameter costs, and the AICc gives the same comparison.

The ecological question is about the size of the effect. The probability of survival is 0.47 with predators (95% Wald interval from 0.43 to 0.51) and 0.93 without them (from 0.90 to 0.95), so the presence of predators lowers it by 0.46, from 0.93 to 0.47. We do not calculate an interval for that difference, because a standard error of a difference of two probabilities that were estimated on the logit scale requires the delta method of Chapter 6.

The evidence is weaker than the difference in AIC suggests. Question 5 showed that the counts of the tanks with predators vary more than the Binomial model allows, so their interval is too narrow and the difference in AIC overstates the evidence. The direction and the size of the effect are still what the data show. This is the first model of the book in which a parameter differs between groups. In Chapter 6 parameters will depend on continuous predictors, and in Chapter 7 on groups of observations.

2 Exercise 2: Glacier lily seedlings in quadrats

The data set Lily_sum in the package emdbook contains counts of the glacier lily Erythronium grandiflorum in a grid of 16 × 16 quadrats of 2 m × 2 m in Washington Gulch, sampled in 1992 (Thomson et al. 1996). We use the column seedlings, the number of seedlings in each of the 256 quadrats. Load the data with data(Lily_sum, package = "emdbook").

Let \(X_i\) be the number of seedlings in quadrat \(i\). We use a Negative Binomial model with mean \(\mu\) and shape \(k\),

\[ X_i \sim \operatorname{NegBin}(\mu, k), \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X_i)=\mu+\frac{\mu^2}{k}, \]

and assume that the quadrats are i.i.d. Neighbouring quadrats of a grid are likely to resemble each other, so this is a simplification, as it was for the visits to owl nests in the Chapter 3 practical (models for observations that are not independent start in Chapter 7).

  1. Estimate \(\mu\) and \(k\) with the method of moments, as in the Chapter 3 practical. Also calculate the fraction of quadrats without seedlings and the largest count.
  2. Write the NLL of the model with \(\log\mu\) and \(\log k\) as parameters and minimise it with optim(), using the method-of-moments estimates as starting values and hessian = TRUE. Report the estimates on the original scale. Compare the log-likelihood at the maximum likelihood estimates with the log-likelihood at the method-of-moments estimates.
  3. Calculate the log-likelihood on a grid of values of \(\mu\) and \(k\) with two nested for loops and draw the contours of the relative log-likelihood with contour(). Add both pairs of estimates and the contour of the approximate joint 95% confidence region. Is the method-of-moments estimate inside the region?
  4. Calculate a 95% Wald interval for \(k\) (on the log scale, with the endpoints transformed back) and a 95% profile likelihood interval for \(k\) with uniroot(). Compare them.
  5. Plot the relative frequencies of the counts together with the probabilities of the fitted Negative Binomial model and of a Poisson model. Compare the fraction of empty quadrats under each model with the observed fraction, and compare the two models with AIC and with its small-sample correction AICc (see Model comparison with AIC).

3 Exercise 3: Empty quadrats and zero inflation

About half of the quadrats of Exercise 2 have no seedlings. The Negative Binomial model describes this with a small shape \(k\). A different explanation is that a fraction of the quadrats cannot have seedlings at all (for example, because no seeds arrive there), while the counts in the rest follow a Poisson distribution. This is a zero-inflated Poisson model (see How to make zero-inflated distributions in the supplement). Its probability mass function is

\[ P(X=x\mid\lambda,p_0)= \begin{cases} p_0+(1-p_0)\,e^{-\lambda}, & x=0,\\[4pt] (1-p_0)\,\dfrac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^{x}}{x!}, & x>0, \end{cases} \]

where \(p_0\) (pzero in the code of the supplement) is the probability that a quadrat cannot have seedlings and \(\lambda\) is the mean number of seedlings in the quadrats that can have them. Use the same counts as in Exercise 2 and the same assumption of i.i.d. quadrats.

  1. Write a function dzipois(x, lambda, pzero, log = FALSE) that returns the probability mass of the zero-inflated Poisson distribution, or its logarithm when log = TRUE. Check that the probabilities add up to one over a wide range of counts, for two combinations of parameter values.
  2. Write the NLL with \(\log\lambda\) and \(\operatorname{logit}p_0\) as parameters. Choose starting values from the data (think of the fraction of empty quadrats and of the mean of the quadrats that are not empty) and fit the model with optim(). Interpret both estimates.
  3. Compare the zero-inflated Poisson model with the Negative Binomial model of Exercise 2 using AIC and AICc. What does the comparison come down to when both models have the same number of parameters?
  4. Plot the probabilities of both fitted models over the relative frequencies of the counts. Under each model, calculate the probability of an empty quadrat and the probability of 20 or more seedlings, and compare them with the observed fractions. What does each model say about how the counts arise? Can the comparison identify the ecological cause of the empty quadrats?

4 Exercise 4: Timber volume and body mass

This exercise fits models to continuous measurements, so the contributions to the likelihood are densities. We use a Gamma model with shape \(k\) and rate \(r\) (shape and rate in R), for which

\[ X_i \sim \operatorname{Gamma}(k, r), \qquad E[X_i]=\frac{k}{r}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X_i)=\frac{k}{r^2}. \]

The first three questions use the data set trees of the Chapter 3 practical, with the measurements of 31 felled black cherry trees. That practical used their heights. Here \(X_i\) is the volume of timber of tree \(i\) in cubic feet (ft³), the column Volume. The last two questions use the data set penguins, which is part of the datasets package in R 4.5.0 and later.1 It contains measurements of adult penguins of three species near Palmer Station, Antarctica (Gorman, Williams and Fraser 2014). There \(X_i\) is the body mass of Adelie penguin \(i\), the column body_mass, in grams. In both cases we assume that the observations are i.i.d.

  1. Load the trees with data(trees, package = "datasets"). Write the NLL of the Gamma model with dgamma(..., log = TRUE) and with \(\log k\) and \(\log r\) as parameters. Calculate method-of-moments estimates from the formulas of the mean and the variance, use them as starting values and fit the model with optim() and hessian = TRUE. Report the estimates of \(k\) and \(r\) with their units and the maximum likelihood estimate of the mean volume.
  2. Calculate the correlation between the estimates of \(\log k\) and \(\log r\) from the Hessian. Calculate the log-likelihood on a grid of values of \(k\) and \(r\) and draw the contours of the relative log-likelihood. What does the tilt of the contours mean?
  3. Calculate a slice and a profile of the log-likelihood for the shape \(k\) and plot both as relative log-likelihoods. Find the range of values of \(k\) for which each curve is within 1.92 units of the maximum. Which of the two ranges is a confidence interval, and why is the slice narrower?
  4. Load the penguins with data(penguins, package = "datasets") and keep the body masses of the Adelie penguins. One of them is missing: remove it explicitly. Fit the Gamma model to the masses in grams and again to the same masses in kilograms. With the masses in grams the NLL is a large number, and the default stopping rule of optim() can end the search too early, so add the argument control = list(reltol = 1e-12) to both fits (if R prints “NaNs produced” during the search, see the callout When a fit fails or prints warnings). Compare the estimates and the maximum log-likelihoods of the two fits. Explain the differences with the units of a density.
  5. Fit a LogNormal model to the body masses with dlnorm() and optim(), in grams and in kilograms, and compare it with the Gamma model using AIC and AICc. Does the comparison depend on the units?

5 Exercise 5: Estimators and intervals across hypothetical experiments

This exercise is extra practice. With a single sample we cannot tell how far an estimate is from the parameter, or whether a confidence interval contains it, because the parameter is unknown. As in Exercise 3 of the Chapter 3 practical, we can study the behaviour of an estimator by simulating hypothetical experiments from a model whose parameters we know. Here we use the maximum likelihood estimates of Exercise 4 as the parameters of that model. The simulation then shows how the estimators and the intervals would behave if the fitted Gamma model had generated the data (see Properties of maximum likelihood estimators and Uncertainty from the likelihood). This exercise continues with the objects of Exercise 4. The function that runs the simulation is given below the questions with three gaps, which you fill in.

  1. For the shape \(k\) of the Gamma model fitted to the tree volumes, calculate a 95% Wald interval (on the log scale, with the endpoints transformed back) and a 95% profile likelihood interval with uniroot(). Compare them.
  2. Complete the first gap of the function simulate_gamma_experiments() below. Set seed 801 and simulate 2,000 hypothetical experiments, each with 31 trees from a Gamma model whose shape and rate are the maximum likelihood estimates of Exercise 4. For each experiment, the function estimates the shape by the method of moments and by maximum likelihood (the fit starts at the method-of-moments estimates and uses control = list(reltol = 1e-12), as in Exercise 4). Compare the mean, the standard deviation and the mean squared error of the two estimators with the shape that you used to simulate. Tip: the function has the sample size, the shape and the rate as arguments because you will need it again in question 4.
  3. Complete the second and the third gap, which record for each experiment whether the Wald interval and the profile likelihood interval for the shape contain the shape that you used to simulate, and calculate the coverage of both intervals. Hint: you do not need uniroot() inside the simulation. A profile likelihood interval contains a value whenever the profile log-likelihood at that value is within qchisq(p = 0.95, df = 1) / 2 of the maximum log-likelihood, so one extra fit with the shape fixed at that value is enough.
  4. Set seed 802 and repeat questions 2 and 3 with experiments of 151 penguins from the Gamma model fitted to the body masses in grams. How do the bias and the coverage change? The two simulations differ in the sample size and in the model. How can you check which of the two explains the change? Do it, without resetting the seed.
  5. Why do these results only approximate the behaviour of the estimators and the intervals for the real trees and penguins?

The function below needs gamma_nll() and gamma_nll_given_shape() from Exercise 4, and the objects critical_value and cutoff (the critical value of the standard Normal distribution and the cutoff of the relative log-likelihood for a 95% interval). It stores the results of every experiment in one row of a data frame. In each gap, replace NA by your own code. The lines that follow a gap use the names of the objects that the gap creates, so keep those names.

# One simulation of hypothetical experiments from a Gamma model whose parameters we know.
# For each experiment: the maximum likelihood and the method-of-moments estimates of the
# shape, and whether the Wald interval (log scale) and the profile interval contain the
# shape that was used to simulate.
simulate_gamma_experiments = function(n_obs, shape, rate, n_experiments) {
  results = data.frame(
    shape_ml = rep(NA, times = n_experiments),
    shape_mom = rep(NA, times = n_experiments),
    wald_covered = rep(NA, times = n_experiments),
    profile_covered = rep(NA, times = n_experiments),
    converged = rep(NA, times = n_experiments)
  )
  for (e in seq_len(n_experiments)) {
    # One hypothetical experiment and its method-of-moments estimates.
    x = rgamma(n = n_obs, shape = shape, rate = rate)
    x_mean = mean(x)
    x_variance = mean((x - x_mean)^2)
    shape_mom = x_mean^2 / x_variance

    # GAP 1: maximum likelihood fit of the Gamma model to x with optim(), started at the
    # method-of-moments estimates of the shape and the rate (on the log scale), with
    # method = "BFGS", hessian = TRUE and control = list(reltol = 1e-12). Store the result
    # in an object called fit.
    fit = NA
    shape_ml = exp(fit$par[1])
    se_log_shape = sqrt(solve(fit$hessian)[1, 1])

    # The Wald interval contains the shape when the estimate is within the critical value
    # of standard errors on the log scale. The profile interval contains it when the
    # relative profile log-likelihood at that shape is within the cutoff.

    # GAP 2: TRUE when the Wald interval contains the shape that was used to simulate.
    wald_covered = NA

    # GAP 3: fit the model with the shape fixed at the shape that was used to simulate,
    # which re-estimates the rate only (use gamma_nll_given_shape() with method = "BFGS"
    # and control = list(reltol = 1e-12), starting at the estimate of log(rate) of fit),
    # and store it in an object called inner_fit. Then record in profile_covered whether the
    # relative profile log-likelihood at that shape is within the cutoff.
    inner_fit = NA
    profile_covered = NA

    results$shape_ml[e] = shape_ml
    results$shape_mom[e] = shape_mom
    results$wald_covered[e] = wald_covered
    results$profile_covered[e] = profile_covered
    results$converged[e] = fit$convergence == 0 && inner_fit$convergence == 0
  }
  results
}

Probability distributions

Before you start, read the entries for the Binomial, Poisson, Negative Binomial, Normal, LogNormal and Beta distributions in the distribution supplement. They give the probability mass or density function, mean, variance and R functions of each distribution, which you will need in the exercises below. The first two exercises are worked examples, one for a discrete and one for a continuous distribution. Work through them before trying the remaining exercises.

The distribution functions of the practical come from the packages RTMB and RTMBdist, which provide the distribution functions used in the whole course, including some that base R lacks. Chapter 4 explains what else they do. Load them once, RTMB first:

# Load RTMB first and RTMBdist second.
library(RTMB)
library(RTMBdist)

1 Exercise 1: Seedling survival

Assume that each of 25 seedlings has probability \(0.65\) of surviving. Assume a Binomial model to describe this process.

  1. Calculate the probability that exactly 15 survive.
  2. Calculate the probability that at least 15 survive.
  3. Calculate the expected number and variance of survivors.
  4. Simulate 1,000 groups of 25 seedlings and compare the observed frequencies with the model probabilities.

1.1 Solution

Assume that each of 25 seedlings has probability \(0.65\) of surviving. Assume a Binomial model to describe this process.

Let

\[ X=\text{the number of seedlings that survive}. \]

The process has:

  • a fixed number of trials, \(n=25\);
  • two possible outcomes for each seedling: survives or does not survive;
  • the same survival probability, \(p=0.65\);
  • independent seedlings.

Therefore,

\[ X\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(n=25,p=0.65). \]

In R, the Binomial distribution uses:

  • dbinom() for probability mass;
  • pbinom() for cumulative probabilities;
  • rbinom() for simulation.
n = 25
p_survive = 0.65

1.1.1 1. Probability that exactly 15 survive

We want to calculate

\[ P(X=15). \]

The Binomial probability mass function is

\[ P(X=x)= \binom{n}{x}p^x(1-p)^{n-x}. \]

Substituting \(x=15\), \(n=25\), and \(p=0.65\) gives

\[ \begin{aligned} P(X=15) &= \binom{25}{15}(0.65)^{15}(1-0.65)^{25-15}\\ &= \binom{25}{15}(0.65)^{15}(0.35)^{10}\\ &= \frac{25!}{15!10!}(0.65)^{15}(0.35)^{10}\\ &\approx 0.14. \end{aligned} \]

Thus, the probability that exactly 15 seedlings survive is approximately 0.14, or 14%.

In R, dbinom() performs this calculation:

prob_exactly_15 = dbinom(
  x = 15,
  size = n,
  prob = p_survive
)

prob_exactly_15
[1] 0.1408523

The combinatorial term can also be calculated directly in R:

choose(25, 15)
[1] 3268760

1.1.2 2. Probability that at least 15 survive

“At least 15” means 15 or more:

\[ P(X\geq 15)=P(X=15)+P(X=16)+\cdots+P(X=25). \]

By using the complement,

\[ P(X\geq 15) = 1-P(X\leq 14). \]

Using the Binomial formula, this is

\[ P(X\geq 15) = 1-\sum_{x=0}^{14} \binom{25}{x}(0.65)^x(0.35)^{25-x}. \]

Evaluating this sum gives

\[ P(X\geq 15)\approx 0.77. \]

Therefore, the probability that at least 15 seedlings survive is approximately 0.77, or 77%.

In R, pbinom() calculates \(P(X\leq x)\) so we take the complement:

prob_at_least_15 = 1 - pbinom(
  q = 14,
  size = n,
  prob = p_survive
)

prob_at_least_15
[1] 0.7711612

The value q = 14 is used because

\[ P(X>14)=P(X\geq 15). \]

1.1.3 3. Expected number and variance of survivors

For a Binomial random variable,

\[ \mu=E[X]=np \]

and

\[ \operatorname{Var}(X)=np(1-p). \]

The expected number of survivors is therefore

\[ \begin{aligned} E[X] &=25(0.65)\\ &=16.25. \end{aligned} \]

The variance is

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(X) &=25(0.65)(1-0.65)\\ &=25(0.65)(0.35)\\ &=5.69. \end{aligned} \]

In R:

expected_survivors = n * p_survive

variance_survivors = n * p_survive * (1 - p_survive)

c(
  expected = expected_survivors,
  variance = variance_survivors
)
expected variance 
 16.2500   5.6875

The expected value, 16.25, does not need to be an integer. It represents the long-run average number of survivors across many groups of 25 seedlings.

1.1.4 4. Simulating 1,000 groups

We can simulate 1,000 groups using rbinom():

set.seed(123)

simulated_survivors = rbinom(
  n = 1000,
  size = n,
  prob = p_survive
)

head(simulated_survivors)
[1] 18 14 17 13 12 20

Each value in simulated_survivors is the number of survivors in one simulated group.

To calculate the observed relative frequency for every possible number of survivors, use table():

support = 0:n

observed_frequency = as.numeric(
  table(factor(simulated_survivors, levels = support))
) / length(simulated_survivors)

model_probability = dbinom(
  x = support,
  size = n,
  prob = p_survive
)

comparison = data.frame(
  survivors = support,
  observed = observed_frequency,
  model = model_probability
)

2 Exercise 2: Bill length under a Normal model

The data set penguins, which is built into R 4.5.0 and later1, contains measurements of adult penguins of three species near Palmer Station, Antarctica (Gorman, Williams and Fraser 2014). One of the measurements is the length of the bill (the column bill_len, in millimetres). The theory used the flipper length of the Gentoo penguins. Here we use the bill length of the 68 Chinstrap penguins (Pygoscelis antarcticus), which is well described by a Normal distribution with a mean of 49 mm and a standard deviation of 3.3 mm (Chapter 3 explains how such values are obtained from a sample).

Suppose the bill length \(X\) of a Chinstrap penguin follows that Normal model:

\[ X\sim\operatorname{Normal}(49,3.3^2). \]

  1. Calculate the probability that a bill is between 45 mm and 52 mm long.
  2. Find the length below which 95% of bills fall under this model.
  3. Simulate 1,000 bill lengths. Compare a density-scaled histogram with the model density and explain any differences.

2.1 Solution

Suppose the bill length \(X\) of a Chinstrap penguin follows a Normal model with mean 49 mm and standard deviation 3.3 mm.

The observational unit is one penguin and bill length is a continuous random variable, so we calculate probabilities for intervals of length. Note that the model above is written with the variance, \(3.3^2\), whereas the sd argument in R takes the standard deviation, 3.3 mm.

In R, the Normal distribution uses:

  • dnorm() for probability density;
  • pnorm() for cumulative probabilities;
  • qnorm() for quantiles;
  • rnorm() for simulation.
# Parameters of the bill length model (in millimetres)
bill_mean = 49
bill_sd = 3.3

2.1.1 1. Probability that a bill is between 45 mm and 52 mm long

The probability of an interval is the cumulative probability at its upper limit minus the cumulative probability at its lower limit:

\[ P(45<X\leq52)=P(X\leq52)-P(X\leq45). \]

In R, pnorm() calculates \(P(X\leq x)\), so we subtract two calls:

# Subtract the probability below 45 mm from the probability below 52 mm
prob_45_to_52 = pnorm(q = 52, mean = bill_mean, sd = bill_sd) -
  pnorm(q = 45, mean = bill_mean, sd = bill_sd)

prob_45_to_52
[1] 0.7056159

Thus, the probability that a bill is between 45 mm and 52 mm long is approximately 0.71, or 71%. Including or excluding the endpoints makes no difference, because the probability of any exact length is zero in a continuous model.

2.1.2 2. Length below which 95% of bills fall

We want the length \(x\) such that

\[ P(X\leq x)=0.95. \]

This is the 95th percentile (i.e., the 0.95 quantile) of the distribution. In R, qnorm() calculates it:

# Quantile of the model at a cumulative probability of 0.95
bill_95 = qnorm(p = 0.95, mean = bill_mean, sd = bill_sd)

bill_95
[1] 54.42802

The length is approximately 54.4 mm. That is, under this model 95% of bills are shorter than 54.4 mm and 5% are longer.

2.1.3 3. Simulating 1,000 bill lengths

We can simulate 1,000 bill lengths using rnorm():

# Set a seed so that the simulated sample is reproducible
set.seed(seed = 101)

bill_lengths = rnorm(n = 1000, mean = bill_mean, sd = bill_sd)

head(bill_lengths)
[1] 47.92408 50.82312 46.77269 49.70739 50.02554 52.87409

Each value in bill_lengths is the bill length of one simulated penguin. We can now plot a histogram of the sample and add the density of the model with curve():

# Histogram on the density scale (probability = TRUE) so that it is comparable to the curve
hist(
  x = bill_lengths, breaks = 25, probability = TRUE,
  col = "grey85", border = "white",
  xlab = "Bill length (mm)", ylab = "Density (1/mm)",
  main = "Simulated bill lengths and Normal model"
)

# Add the density of the model that generated the sample
curve(
  dnorm(x = x, mean = bill_mean, sd = bill_sd),
  add = TRUE, lwd = 2
)

legend(
  "topright", legend = "Normal density",
  lty = 1, lwd = 2, bty = "n"
)

The histogram uses density scaling because the curve also shows density (with counts on the vertical axis the two would not be comparable). The bars do not match the curve exactly because they summarise one sample of 1,000 simulated penguins, and their heights also depend on the width of the bins. With a larger number of simulated penguins, the histogram would usually become closer to the model density.

5 Exercise 5: Body mass under a LogNormal model

Suppose body mass \(X\) in grams follows a LogNormal model with meanlog = log(30) and sdlog = 0.5. These two parameters are the mean and the standard deviation of the logarithm of body mass. The formulae for the median and the mean are in the LogNormal entry of the supplement.

  1. Calculate the median and the mean body mass of the model. Explain why they differ.
  2. Calculate the probability that body mass exceeds 50 g and the 95th percentile.

6 Exercise 6: Vegetation cover under a Beta model

Suppose \(X\) is the fraction of a quadrat covered by vegetation, measured as a continuous variable, and that \(X\) follows a Beta model with \(a=2\) and \(b=5\). In R these parameters are shape1 and shape2. The formulae for the mean and the variance are in the Beta entry of the supplement.

  1. Calculate the mean and the variance of the model. Express the mean as a percentage of the quadrat.
  2. Calculate \(P(X<0.20)\) and the 95th percentile of cover. Explain both results.
